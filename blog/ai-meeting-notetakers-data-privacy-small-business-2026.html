AI Meeting Notetakers Are Recording More Than the Meeting (2026)

Ulises Paiz, Owner, Ghosxt

Ulises Paiz is the owner and sole engineer at Ghosxt, a Salinas, CA managed IT and cybersecurity provider. He holds an M.S. in Cybersecurity and Information Assurance (WGU, 2026) and nine industry certifications including CompTIA SecurityX, CySA+, and Microsoft AZ-104, with prior DoD and federal contractor infrastructure experience. More about Ulises →

TL;DR: AI notetakers built into Zoom, Microsoft Teams, and similar tools now transcribe and summarize calls by default, then store that transcript somewhere outside the meeting itself. The fix is treating notetaker output as stored business data with its own consent, retention, and access rules, not as a convenience feature that ships turned on.

Three things to decide before AI notetakers stay on by default.

  1. Name which meetings are off-limits, starting with anything involving client financial detail or legal matters.
  2. Set a retention period for transcripts instead of letting them accumulate indefinitely in chat and file-sharing apps.
  3. Tell participants before a call is transcribed, the same way you would tell them about a recording.

If your current AI policy does not mention meeting notetakers by name, most employees will assume the vendor default is the approved setting.

Open a Zoom or Microsoft Teams call today and there is a good chance an AI notetaker joins automatically, without anyone clicking a button. It listens to the whole call, produces a transcript, writes a summary, and often posts that summary into a chat channel or shared drive a few minutes after the meeting ends. That is a genuinely useful feature. It is also a new place where sensitive conversations end up stored, searchable, and shared, usually without anyone treating it as a data-handling decision at all.

1. A transcript remembers everything a person would forget

A meeting notetaker does not summarize the way a person would. It captures the whole conversation, including the aside about a client's cash-flow problem, the phone number read out loud, or the offhand comment about an employee issue that no one intended to write down. That transcript then lives in a searchable archive, often synced automatically into a chat app or cloud drive the same way files from OneDrive, SharePoint, or Teams already do, which means it inherits whatever access and retention gaps already exist in those tools.

2. Consent is a real requirement, not a formality

Telling participants that a call is being transcribed is not just good practice, it can be a legal requirement depending on the state and who is on the call. A vendor default that joins silently, or a meeting host who never mentions it, puts the business in the position of having recorded a conversation no one agreed to. This is the same category of gap covered in our post on shadow AI tools employees adopt on their own, except here the tool is often already built into software the business is already paying for, which makes it easy to assume it was already reviewed.

3. This belongs in the written AI policy, not left to each meeting host

The most durable fix is deciding, in writing, which meetings a notetaker should never join, how long transcripts are kept before they are deleted, and who can see them after the meeting ends. A written AI acceptable use policy should name meeting notetakers specifically, the same way a data retention and destruction policy should cover transcripts alongside email and files, rather than leaving the vendor's default setting as the business's actual policy.

Where to start this week

Check whether Zoom AI Companion, Teams recap, or a similar feature is turned on by default in your tenant, then confirm where the output goes and who can open it. Most small businesses find the feature was enabled automatically during a platform update, not chosen deliberately, which is exactly the kind of setting worth reviewing alongside the rest of a Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace security check.

Frequently asked questions

What does an AI meeting notetaker actually capture?

Most AI notetakers built into Zoom, Microsoft Teams, and similar platforms record audio and video, generate a full transcript, and produce a written summary, usually stored in the vendor's cloud or synced into a chat or file-sharing app separate from the meeting itself.

Do all participants need to consent to an AI notetaker joining a call?

Requirements vary by state and by who is on the call, so treat it as a consent question every time. At minimum, tell participants an AI notetaker is present before the call starts recording, the same way you would disclose a recording.

Is an AI meeting transcript the same risk as an email?

Often a bigger one. A transcript captures everything said on a call, including details no one intended to put in writing, then stores it in a searchable place that may sync automatically without anyone reviewing it first.

Should a small business turn AI notetakers off entirely?

Not necessarily. Decide which meetings are off-limits, set a retention period for transcripts instead of letting them accumulate indefinitely, and name AI notetakers specifically in the written AI acceptable use policy.
