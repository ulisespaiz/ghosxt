Getting Bounce-Back Emails You Did Not Send? What It Means and What to Do

Ulises Paiz, Owner, Ghosxt

Ulises Paiz is the owner and sole engineer at Ghosxt, a Salinas, CA managed IT and cybersecurity provider. He holds an M.S. in Cybersecurity and Information Assurance (WGU, 2026) and nine industry certifications including CompTIA SecurityX, CySA+, and Microsoft AZ-104, with prior DoD and federal contractor infrastructure experience. TL;DR: Bounce-back emails for messages you never sent usually mean a spammer forged your address, not that your mailbox was broken into. Check your Sent folder, sign-in history and mailbox rules to be sure, do not open anything attached to the notices, and set up SPF, DKIM and DMARC so forged mail gets rejected.

Five steps when bounce-backs arrive for mail you never sent.

  1. Do not open attachments or links. Some fake failure notices are phishing.
  2. Check your Sent folder. Look for messages you did not write.
  3. Review sign-in history. Look for devices and locations that are not yours.
  4. Check mailbox rules and forwarding. Remove anything you did not create.
  5. Tell IT. Ask whether your domain enforces SPF, DKIM and DMARC.

If you run a small business in Monterey County or anywhere on the Central Coast, this may be how a Monday starts: an inbox with dozens of "Undeliverable" or "Mail delivery failed" notices for emails nobody at your company sent. Bounce-back emails you did not send mean one of two things. Either a spammer forged your address on their own mail, or someone sent mail from inside your mailbox. The first is a nuisance. The second is a break-in.

This guide covers why the notices arrive, how to tell in ten minutes whether your account was actually used, and how to stop your address from being forged.

1. Why you get bounce-backs for mail you never sent

Email lets a sender write any address in the From line, the same way anyone can write any return address on an envelope. Spammers use real addresses because they look more believable than made-up ones. When their messages hit closed or full mailboxes, the receiving servers send the failure notice to the address in the From line. That is you.

The term for this is backscatter. It needs no access to your account, your password or your computer. Your address may have been copied from your website, an old data leak or a contact's address book.

The less common cause is worse. If someone has your password, or tricked you into approving a sign-in, they can send spam or fake invoices from your real mailbox. Some of those bounce too, and the notices look exactly the same.

2. How to tell if your account was actually used

Check these before deciding it is only spoofing.

  • Sent folder and Deleted Items. Messages you did not write are proof the mailbox was used. An empty Sent folder is not proof of the opposite, because intruders often delete what they send.
  • Sign-in history. Microsoft 365 and Google Workspace both list recent sign-ins. A device, city or country you do not recognize settles the question.
  • Mailbox rules and forwarding. Intruders add rules that hide replies or copy mail elsewhere. See our post on hidden email forwarding rules for where to look.
  • Sending blocks. If your provider has stopped you from sending because of "unusual activity" or a sending limit, the mail came from your account.
  • Replies from real contacts. Customers asking about a strange invoice or shared file from you point to a compromised mailbox, or to a lookalike domain.

If any check comes back positive, change the password, sign out of all sessions, turn on multi-factor authentication, and warn the contacts who received mail. Our guide on what to do after clicking a phishing link has the full first-hour list.

One caution either way: do not open attachments or click "release message" links inside a failure notice. Fake bounce-backs are a common phishing lure. And if the notices arrive alongside thousands of sign-up confirmations, read our post on email bombing, which is a different attack with a different purpose.

3. How to stop your address from being forged

You cannot stop a spammer from typing your address. You can tell every other mail server what to do when they try.

  • Publish SPF and DKIM. These records list which servers may send for your domain and add a signature that proves a message is real.
  • Enforce DMARC. A DMARC policy of quarantine or reject tells receivers to refuse mail that fails those checks. A policy of "none" only reports and blocks nothing. Our guide to SPF, DKIM and DMARC walks through the setup.
  • Read the DMARC reports. They show who is sending as your domain, including tools you forgot about, so you can enforce without breaking your own mail.
  • Wait out the rest. A backscatter run usually fades within days once the spammer moves to another address. A temporary inbox rule for failure notices keeps real mail visible in the meantime.

Enforcement also protects your customers. Forged mail in your name is how business email compromise reaches the people who trust you.

The takeaway

Bounce-backs for mail you never sent are usually someone borrowing your name, not your mailbox. Spend ten minutes proving that, because the two cases call for very different responses. Then close the gap that let your address be forged. Frequently asked questions

Why am I getting bounce-back emails for messages I did not send?

Usually because a spammer put your email address in the From line of their own messages. When those messages hit dead addresses, the failure notices come back to you. This is called backscatter. Less often, it means someone signed in to your mailbox and sent mail from it.

Do undeliverable emails I never sent mean my email was hacked?

Not by themselves. Forging a From address takes no access to your account. Your account was likely used if the messages are in your Sent folder, your sign-in history shows a device or location you do not recognize, or your email provider has blocked you from sending.

Should I open the attachment in a mail delivery failed message?

No. A real failure notice can be read without opening anything, and some messages dressed up as failure notices are phishing. Do not open attachments or click links that offer to release, resend or review a message.

How do I stop bounce-back emails from spoofed messages?

Publish SPF, DKIM and DMARC records for your domain and move DMARC to an enforcing policy. Receiving mail servers can then reject forged messages outright, which cuts both the spoofing and the failure notices that follow.

Should I change my password if I get bounce-backs I did not send?

Change it if you find any sign the mailbox itself was used, or if you cannot rule it out. Use a password you use nowhere else, sign out of all sessions, and turn on multi-factor authentication.
