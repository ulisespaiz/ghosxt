Break-Glass Admin Account for Microsoft 365: Set It Up (2026)

TL;DR: A break-glass account is a sealed emergency admin login that gets you back into Microsoft 365 when MFA or a sign-in policy locks everyone out. Create two, protect them with hardware keys, and test them every quarter.

Three steps for this week.

  1. Create two cloud-only admin accounts. Give them neutral names, not a person's name, and no mailbox or license.
  2. Protect each with a hardware security key. Store the keys and a long password in two separate secure places.
  3. Set an alert on every sign-in. Any use of these accounts should notify you at once, and you should test them quarterly.

If you run Microsoft 365 for a small business in Monterey County or anywhere else, there is a lockout scenario most owners never plan for: nobody can sign in as an administrator. A phone is lost, a multi-factor prompt stops working, or a new security rule blocks every admin at once. Without a way back in, you wait on a support case while email and files stay out of reach.

A break-glass account is the fix. This guide explains what one is, how to set it up safely, and how to keep it from becoming a risk of its own.

1. What a break-glass account is and why you need one

It is an emergency administrator login that you do not use day to day. Think of the glass case with a fire axe: sealed, labeled, and only opened in an emergency.

  • It survives a policy mistake. A single misconfigured Conditional Access rule can block every admin, including the one who wrote it. The emergency account sits outside that rule.
  • It survives a lost phone. If your only admin relies on a phone for multi-factor authentication and it is gone, you still have a path in.
  • It survives a missing person. If the one person who knows the admin password is away or leaves, the business keeps control of its own tenant.
  • It is not for daily use. Using it for routine work defeats the purpose and buries the alert that tells you something is wrong.

2. How to set it up safely

An account with full control is exactly what an attacker wants, so the setup matters as much as the account.

  • Make two, cloud-only. Create them in Microsoft 365 itself, not synced from a local directory, so an on-site failure cannot lock them out.
  • Use the default domain and a neutral name. Do not use an obvious name such as "admin" or a person's name, and do not attach a mailbox or a license.
  • Use a long random password. Generate at least 24 characters, print it, and seal it in a safe or safe-deposit box. Do not keep it in the same password manager your admins sign in to every day.
  • Add a hardware key. Register a security key as the sign-in method, since it does not depend on a phone. Our guide to passkeys and passwordless sign-in explains why this is stronger than a text code.
  • Limit who can reach the sealed credentials. Two trusted people, written down, and a note of who they are in your incident response plan.

3. How to monitor and test it

A break-glass account that nobody watches or tries is a hidden risk. Two habits keep it useful.

  • Alert on every sign-in. Because the account should almost never be used, any sign-in is worth an immediate message to you. Build it on your audit logs and send it to a place people actually read.
  • Test every quarter. Sign in, confirm the access works, confirm the alert fires, then seal everything again. Test after any change to sign-in policies too.
  • Review who holds admin rights. The emergency accounts are one part of a bigger picture. Pair this with a look at privileged access so you know every account with full control.
  • Write down the steps. When the day comes, nobody should be reading instructions for the first time. Put the procedure in your business continuity plan.

The takeaway

Strong sign-in rules are good, and they also create a new way to lock yourself out. A pair of sealed, monitored, regularly tested emergency accounts costs very little and removes the worst version of that problem. Frequently asked questions

What is a break-glass account in Microsoft 365?

A break-glass account is an emergency administrator login that you keep sealed and only use when normal admin sign-in fails. It lets you get back into your tenant if multi-factor authentication breaks, a Conditional Access rule locks everyone out, or your regular admins are unavailable.

How many break-glass accounts does a small business need?

Two is the usual answer. A second account covers the case where one has a problem, and storing them in different places means a single mistake does not remove both. Keep the count small, because every extra powerful account is more to protect.

Should a break-glass account use multi-factor authentication?

Yes, but not one tied to a person's phone. Use a phishing-resistant method such as a hardware security key, kept in a secure location. A very long random password alone is not enough, since this account holds full control of your tenant.

How often should I test the break-glass account?

Test it every quarter and after any change to sign-in policies. A sealed account that nobody has tried is a guess. Confirm that it signs in, then confirm the alert fires and the password and key are still where you expect.

Can I use my normal admin account instead?

No. The point is to have a login that does not depend on the same phone, policies, or person as your day-to-day admin. If your regular admin account is the one that is locked out, it cannot also be the way back in.
