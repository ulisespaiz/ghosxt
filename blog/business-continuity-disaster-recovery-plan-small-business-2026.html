Business Continuity and Disaster Recovery Plan for Small Business: What Belongs In It (2026)

Ulises Paiz, Owner, Ghosxt

Ulises Paiz is the owner and sole engineer at Ghosxt, a Salinas, CA managed IT and cybersecurity provider. He holds an M.S. in Cybersecurity and Information Assurance (WGU, 2026) and nine industry certifications including CompTIA SecurityX, CySA+, and Microsoft AZ-104, with prior DoD and federal contractor infrastructure experience. More about Ulises →

TL;DR: A backup and an incident response plan are not a business continuity and disaster recovery plan. A usable BC/DR plan names the systems the business can't run without, how long it can survive without each one, and who does what when something breaks.

A BC/DR plan needs four things to actually work under pressure.

  1. A short list of the systems the business truly can't operate without, ranked in the order they need to come back.
  2. An honest downtime tolerance for each one, in hours or days, not a number picked because it sounds reassuring.
  3. Named roles: who declares an emergency, who executes recovery steps, and who communicates with staff and clients while it's happening.
  4. An annual tabletop test, so the plan gets exercised before a real outage is the first time anyone reads it.

If your current plan is "we have backups," this is the layer of decision-making that sits on top of them.

A server fails, a water line breaks over the office on a Saturday, or a key vendor shuts down with no warning. In the first hour, the question is not whether backups exist somewhere. It's who is in charge, what gets restored first, and how long the business can actually go without each system before real damage sets in. Most small businesses that have never written this down assume the answer would be obvious in the moment. It usually isn't, and the businesses that have thought it through on paper recover faster than the ones improvising for the first time during an actual outage.

1. A backup, an incident response plan, and a BC/DR plan are three different things

These get used interchangeably, and the mix-up leaves gaps. A backup is a copy of data; it's a tool, not a plan. An incident response plan is specifically about a security event: detecting it, containing it, and working out what happened. A business continuity and disaster recovery plan is broader than either. It covers any disruption, a cyberattack, a fire, a prolonged power outage, or a critical vendor going out of business, and answers a different question than the other two: how does the business keep operating, or get operating again, no matter what caused the stoppage. A company can have excellent backups and a solid incident response plan and still have no answer for what happens the moment the building itself is unusable.

2. What actually belongs in the plan

The plan should start with a short, honest list: the handful of systems and functions the business genuinely cannot operate without, whether that's email, a point-of-sale system, a shared file server, or a specific piece of line-of-business software. Rank them in the order they need to come back online, because trying to restore everything at once usually means nothing comes back quickly. Next to each one, write down how long the business can actually tolerate it being down, a real number based on how the business works, not an aspirational one. Then name roles, not just tasks: who has the authority to declare an emergency and start the plan, who carries out the technical recovery steps, and who is responsible for telling staff, clients, and vendors what's happening while it's underway. A plan with tasks but no names attached tends to stall while everyone assumes someone else is handling it.

3. A plan that has never been read out loud has gaps nobody has found yet

A written plan sitting in a folder is not the same as a plan that works. The gap between the two usually only shows up when someone tries to follow it during a real event, which is the worst possible time to discover it's missing a step. A tabletop exercise closes that gap cheaply: once a year, sit down with the people named in the plan and walk through a specific scenario out loud, a ransomware attack, a flooded office, a key vendor disappearing, and follow the plan step by step as written. It's common to find that a named contact left the company, a restoration step assumes access nobody currently has, or the communication step was never actually assigned to anyone. Finding that during a one-hour meeting is a very different experience than finding it during an actual outage.

Where to start this week

Write down the three to five systems the business truly cannot run without, and next to each one, a realistic number for how long it can be down before it's a serious problem. That short list, plus a named person for who declares an emergency and who handles communication, covers most of what a BC/DR plan needs to start being useful. A written BC/DR plan is also one piece of the broader written policy suite, alongside a WISP, an incident response plan, and an access control and acceptable use policy, that a managed IT relationship should already be maintaining for you rather than leaving you to draft alone.

Frequently asked questions

What is a business continuity and disaster recovery (BC/DR) plan?

A written document naming the systems and functions a business can't operate without, how long it can survive without each one, and the specific steps and named roles for keeping the business running or getting it back up during an outage. It covers more than IT failures, including power loss, an unusable building, or a key vendor going down.

Is a BC/DR plan the same thing as a backup?

No. A backup is one tool the plan relies on. The plan is the decision-making document: which systems get restored first, how long each can be down, who declares an emergency, and who communicates with staff and clients during recovery. Solid backups don't replace having a plan for the moment something actually breaks.

How is a BC/DR plan different from an incident response plan?

An incident response plan covers a security event specifically: detecting it, containing it, and working out what happened. A BC/DR plan covers keeping the business operating no matter what caused the disruption. They should share a contact list and a decision-maker, but only a security incident needs the incident response plan, while a burst pipe or a vendor shutting down needs the BC/DR plan instead.

How often should a small business test its BC/DR plan?

At least once a year, as a tabletop exercise: walk through a specific scenario out loud with the people named in the plan and find where it breaks down before a real outage forces the question. An untested plan usually has gaps that only surface when someone tries to follow it under pressure.
