Calendar Invite Phishing: How Fake Meeting Requests Trick Small Teams

Ulises Paiz, Owner, Ghosxt

He holds an M.S. in Cybersecurity and Information Assurance (WGU, 2026) and nine industry certifications including CompTIA SecurityX, CySA+, and Microsoft AZ-104, with prior DoD and federal contractor infrastructure experience. TL;DR: A fake calendar invite can land on your phone without anyone opening an email. It carries a bad link or phone number dressed as a routine meeting. Stop automatic adding of unknown invites, limit what outsiders see, report oddities, and keep MFA on.

Five checks before you click an invite.

  1. Know the organizer. Unknown sender, no click.
  2. Ignore urgency. "Final notice" is pressure, not scheduling.
  3. Never sign in to view. A real invite needs no password.
  4. Hover the link. Odd domains mean fake.
  5. Report it. Tell your IT provider so the sender is blocked.

If you run a small business in Monterey County or anywhere on the Central Coast, your calendar is now an attack surface. A fake meeting request can appear on your phone with a link, a phone number, and a sense of urgency, and nobody had to open an email.

This guide explains how calendar invite phishing works, what it looks like, and three settings and habits that shut it down for a small team.

1. How a fake invite gets onto your calendar

Calendar systems are built to be helpful. When someone sends an invitation, many setups add it straight to your calendar and raise a notification. Attackers use that convenience.

  • Auto-add. Default settings can place an event on your calendar before you accept anything.
  • Believable titles. Common bait is "Invoice overdue", "Payroll update", "Security alert" or "Shared document review".
  • Links in the description. The event body holds a link to a fake sign-in page, or a "call this number to cancel" line that leads to a callback phishing script.
  • Trusted-looking sender. Attackers often spoof a vendor, a bank, or a real coworker, and use a hijacked account when they can get one.

The result looks like routine work, sitting between a real staff meeting and a real client call.

2. Signs a meeting request is fake

Most fake invites give themselves away if you slow down for ten seconds. Check these before you click anything.

  • You do not know the organizer. A real meeting comes from someone you work with or expect to hear from.
  • It creates urgency. Words like "immediate", "final notice" or "action required" are pressure, not scheduling.
  • It asks you to sign in to view. A real invite does not need a password. See fake e-signature requests for the same trick.
  • The link domain is odd. Hover to check the address. Lookalike names are covered in our post on lookalike domains.
  • The time makes no sense. Events at 3 a.m. or with no attendees besides you are a red flag.

If you are unsure, do not click. Contact the person on a known phone number or ask your IT provider.

3. Three settings and habits that stop it

You do not need new software. You need a few settings and one team rule.

  • Stop automatic adding. In Outlook and Microsoft 365, and in Google Calendar, change the setting so invitations from unknown senders are not added until you respond. Your IT provider can set this for everyone at once.
  • Hide details from outsiders. Limit who can see event details on your calendar. Attackers use public meeting titles to build believable requests, a theme in our business email compromise guide.
  • Report, do not just delete. Teach staff to report a bad invite so the IT provider can block the sender. See building a report habit.
  • Use MFA everywhere. If someone does enter a password on a fake page, multi-factor authentication gives you a second chance.

The takeaway

A calendar invite feels safer than an email, and attackers know it. Turn off automatic adding, limit what outsiders can see, report anything odd, and keep MFA on. Frequently asked questions

What is calendar invite phishing?

It is a scam where an attacker sends a calendar invitation containing a malicious link or a fake phone number. The invite can appear on your calendar automatically, even if you never opened the email.

Can a calendar invite infect my computer?

The invite itself rarely does. The danger is the link or attachment inside it, which leads to a fake login page or a malware download. Treat every unexpected invite as an unexpected email.

How do I stop strangers adding events to my calendar?

In Microsoft 365 and Google Workspace you can turn off automatic adding of invitations from unknown senders, so events only appear after you accept them.

What should I do if I clicked a link in a calendar invite?

Do not enter a password. Close the page, tell your IT provider right away, and change your password and check MFA if you did sign in.

Why do scammers use calendar invites instead of email?

Invites bypass many habits. They show on your phone as a notification, look like routine work, and often skip spam filters because they come as calendar data.
