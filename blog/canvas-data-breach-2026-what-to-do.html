The Canvas Data Breach: What Happened and How to Protect Your Information

Ulises Paiz, Owner, Ghosxt

Ulises Paiz is the owner and sole engineer at Ghosxt, a Salinas, CA managed IT and cybersecurity provider. He holds an M.S. in Cybersecurity and Information Assurance (WGU, 2026) and nine industry certifications including CompTIA SecurityX, CySA+, and Microsoft AZ-104, with prior DoD and federal contractor infrastructure experience. More about Ulises →

If you or your kids use Canvas at school, or you teach or work at a school that runs it, this is where the 2026 Instructure Canvas breach stands, what was exposed, and what to do about it. The post first went up on May 8, 2026, during the outage, and was updated on November 6, 2026 with what Instructure and the U.S. Department of Education have confirmed since. For schools and businesses that depend on hosted platforms, it is also a reminder to review how cloud services are configured and monitored.

Where it stands now

  • The forensic review is done. In its September 29, 2026 update, Instructure says its forensic review of the data involved is complete and that it is delivering each affected institution's data to that school's designated security contacts. Further updates go directly to affected institutions.
  • Individual notices run through the schools. Instructure says it will coordinate notifications to affected users on each school's behalf through its consultant, Kroll. If you were affected, expect to hear from your school or through that process, not from a stranger.
  • Instructure made a deal with the attackers. On May 11, Instructure said it reached an agreement with the unauthorized actor: the data was returned, it received digital confirmation that the data was destroyed, and it was told no Instructure customers would be extorted. The Record reported the deal as a ransom payment. A criminal group's promise to delete data cannot be checked, so treat the data as exposed anyway.
  • Congress asked questions. The chairman of the House Homeland Security Committee wrote to Instructure on May 11 asking for a briefing on both intrusions.

What actually happened

The U.S. Department of Education's alert says Instructure confirmed attackers got into Canvas through Free-For-Teacher accounts, and that the extortion group ShinyHunters claimed responsibility and repeatedly defaced Canvas login pages with ransom demands. Instructure has since discontinued the Free-for-Teacher service.

There were two waves. The Record reports the group stole data at the start of May and came back on May 7 to deface the platform with a ransom message. Instructure took Canvas offline, which is why students at schools across the country lost access to their courses during finals week. ShinyHunters claimed the theft covered about 9,000 Instructure customers. Instructure has not published a count of its own.

What was exposed

According to the Department of Education alert, the attackers had access to:

  • Usernames of students, teachers, and staff.
  • Email addresses tied to Canvas accounts.
  • Course names and enrollment information showing what classes a person took or taught.
  • Messages sent inside Canvas. This is the one that matters most: student and teacher conversations, and the alert notes that some messages may incidentally include personal information people typed into them.

ShinyHunters also claimed to have student ID numbers. That is the attackers' claim, not Instructure's.

What was not exposed

The same alert says Instructure stated there is no evidence that passwords, dates of birth, government identifiers, or financial information were exposed.

So for most people the practical risk is not identity theft in the classic sense. It is targeted phishing, fraud, and impersonation built on the names, emails, courses, and message contents that did get out, plus anything sensitive someone wrote in a message.

What this actually puts you at risk for

Three things, in order.

1. Highly convincing phishing

Anyone holding a copy of this data has names, school email addresses, the schools those people attend, the courses they took, and the names of the professors and teachers they actually talked to. Expect emails and texts that look like they come from a specific professor, a specific TA, or a specific course, with a link to "your final grade," "your scholarship application," or "your transcript." Expect fake breach notices too: "claim your Canvas settlement" or "confirm your identity to receive your breach report." The detail will be unusually right.

2. Account takeover on other services

There is no evidence Canvas passwords were taken. The email address tied to the account is the exposed part. If that email's password is reused on other sites, and any of those sites had a breach, attackers can try those credentials against your inbox. Email is the master key to most of someone's digital life. Defending it is the highest-value thing you can do.

3. Impersonation and "smishing" of family members

This is the one parents miss. If your kid's school email and name are out, scam texts to mom and dad now have a believable hook. "Hi mom this is Sarah, I am locked out of my Canvas account, I need you to send me a code." With enough breach data, an attacker can do that without ever talking to your kid.

What to do now

Practical, in priority order. Most of this is free.

  1. Turn on multi-factor authentication on the email tied to your Canvas account. If you use Gmail, log in, go to Security, turn on 2-Step Verification with the Google Authenticator app. If you use Outlook or Microsoft 365, do the same with the Microsoft Authenticator. Do not use SMS as your only second factor; SMS gets sim-swapped. This is the single highest-leverage thing on this list.
  2. Change your password on any site where you reused that email's password. If you have ever typed the same password into Canvas, your email, a streaming service, and a random forum, fix it now. Use a password manager and let it generate a unique password for every site.
  3. Tell your family the rule. No one in the family sends a code, password, or money based on a text or DM, ever, without calling the person on a known phone number first. If your kid is in college, agree on a code word that has to be spoken on the phone before money moves. This is the cheapest and most effective scam defense there is.
  4. Treat "Canvas" and breach-notice emails with suspicion. Real notices come through your school. If a message asks you to click a link to "restore your account," "verify your data," or "claim a refund," assume it is fake. Open Canvas or your school's site by typing the address yourself.
  5. Watch the school's official communications. Don't trust forwarded screenshots in group chats. Go to the school's actual website or sign in to your student or parent portal directly. If a genuine notice offers free credit or identity monitoring, sign up; it costs nothing and catches future weirdness.
  6. If you are a teacher or administrator, think about what you wrote in Canvas messages. Anything you sent in a Canvas message about a student's mental health, accommodations, family situation, or grades may be in the data. Your school's privacy office and counsel need to know which conversations were sensitive so they can decide whether individual notification is required.

What schools and districts need to do

If you run IT or compliance for a school, district, or campus, you have a different list. If your internal team is already stretched thin, a managed IT services partner can help turn the checklist into repeatable operations. Briefly:

  • Get your data packet. Instructure sends each institution's files through a permissioned ShareFile link, only to the security contacts the institution designated. Confirm your contacts are current and that the packet has been opened and reviewed. Anyone else offering to send you "your Canvas data" is a phishing attempt.
  • Decide on notification. Use the packet to identify affected users, then decide with counsel whether to use Instructure's Kroll-coordinated notices or send your own. Depending on the state, FERPA and student privacy laws may apply. California public schools should be talking to counsel about Civ. Code 1798.29 obligations.
  • Follow the Department of Education's checklist. The alert asks schools to rotate local Canvas integrations, LTI tools, SSO connectors, and API keys; review logs for unusual access, especially between April 25 and May 8, 2026; enforce MFA everywhere; remove unmanaged teacher-created or free accounts; and validate third-party data-sharing agreements.
  • Enable, or verify, MFA on every Canvas admin account and on the underlying identity provider.

The lesson for any business that uses cloud platforms

Canvas is not the only software-as-a-service platform that holds a giant pool of data about you. If you run a small business, you almost certainly have a Microsoft 365 tenant, a CRM, a payroll provider, a file-sharing service, and a handful of other vendors that each hold a different slice of what someone needs to impersonate you. Any one of them can have a Canvas-style week. The Canvas breach is a useful reminder of three things:

  • Your vendors' security is part of your security. You inherited their controls when you bought their service. If they get breached, your data goes with them. Pick vendors who can answer security questions in writing.
  • MFA on the email account is the single most important control in your business. Every other defense gets weaker if attackers can read your inbox.
  • Assume your name and email are already in a dump somewhere. Plan for the phishing that follows, and train people on it. Breach data gets combined with other leaks and reused against the same people.
