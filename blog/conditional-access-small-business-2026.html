Conditional Access for Small Business: What It Blocks and How to Set It Up (2026)

Ulises Paiz, Owner, Ghosxt

TL;DR: a correct password is not the same as a sign-in that looks right.

Conditional Access judges the sign-in, not just the credentials.

  1. It evaluates location, device, and risk on every sign-in attempt, then allows it, challenges it, or blocks it before MFA even comes into play.
  2. A handful of policies cover most of the risk: blocking legacy authentication, requiring MFA everywhere, and requiring a managed device for email and files.
  3. It can lock out an administrator if a policy is misconfigured, which is why report-only mode and a break-glass account are not optional steps.

This builds on our MFA post (which type of second factor to use) and pairs with mobile device management (what makes a device "compliant" in the first place).

A stolen password paired with a correct MFA code still gets someone in the door, and that combination happens more often than most small business owners assume, usually through a phishing page that relays both in real time. A password and MFA prove who is signing in. They say nothing about whether the sign-in itself looks right: a login from a country the business has never operated in, on a personal laptop nobody in the company issued, at three in the morning. Conditional Access is the Microsoft 365 feature that looks at those details and makes a decision before access is granted, and it is one of the pieces behind the Microsoft 365 hardening we set up for clients alongside Intune and Defender for Business.

1. What Conditional Access actually evaluates

A Conditional Access policy is a rule built from conditions and an action. The conditions can include the user or group signing in, the application being accessed, the country or network the request is coming from, whether the device is enrolled and compliant, and a risk score Microsoft calculates from the sign-in's own behavior. The action can be to allow access, require MFA, require a compliant device, block the sign-in entirely, or limit what the session can do once inside, such as blocking downloads on an unmanaged device. Nothing here replaces a password or MFA; it decides when either one is required and what happens around them.

2. The policies that cover most of the risk

A small business does not need dozens of Conditional Access policies to get most of the benefit. A short list handles the bulk of it. Blocking legacy authentication protocols closes a real gap, since older protocols like POP and IMAP were built before MFA existed and can bypass it entirely if left enabled. Requiring MFA for every user, with a stricter requirement such as a hardware key for administrator accounts, extends the MFA guidance already in place. Requiring a compliant, managed device before someone can open company email or files keeps a personal phone or an unknown laptop from reaching data even with the right password, which is where device management and Conditional Access work together. Blocking or challenging sign-ins from countries the business has no reason to operate in removes a large share of automated attack traffic without affecting a single real employee.

3. Rolling it out without locking out your own team

Conditional Access can absolutely lock an administrator out of their own tenant if a policy is written too broadly and turned on without testing. Two habits prevent that. First, every new policy should run in report-only mode, which shows exactly who it would have affected without actually blocking anyone, before it gets switched to enforced. Second, at least one break-glass administrator account should be excluded from every policy and its credentials stored somewhere separate from normal use, so there is always a way back in if something else gets misconfigured. Roll policies out to a small pilot group first, watch the sign-in logs for a few days, and only then widen them to everyone.

It works best as one layer, not the whole plan

Conditional Access is strongest as part of the same identity hardening covered in our identity hardening post, alongside MFA, device compliance, and the phishing-resistant sign-in methods described in our passkeys post. None of those pieces on their own stops every attack, but together they mean a stolen password is rarely enough by itself.

Frequently asked questions

What is Conditional Access?

Conditional Access is a Microsoft 365 feature that evaluates every sign-in against a set of conditions, such as location, device, and risk level, and then allows it, challenges it with extra verification, or blocks it. It sits on top of a password and MFA rather than replacing either one.

Do I need a specific Microsoft 365 plan to use Conditional Access?

Conditional Access requires Microsoft Entra ID P1, which is included in Microsoft 365 Business Premium. Plans below that tier do not include it.

Will Conditional Access lock me out of my own account?

It can, if a policy is written too broadly and applied without testing. The two safeguards are running new policies in report-only mode before enforcing them, and keeping at least one break-glass admin account excluded from every policy so an administrator can always get back in.

How is Conditional Access different from MFA?

MFA asks for a second proof of identity at every sign-in. Conditional Access decides when that proof is required, whether a device also needs to be managed, and whether a sign-in should be blocked outright based on where and how it is happening. MFA is one of the actions a Conditional Access policy can require.
