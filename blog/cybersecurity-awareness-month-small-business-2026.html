Cybersecurity Awareness Month 2026: A Practical Checklist for Small Business

Ulises Paiz, Owner, Ghosxt

Ulises Paiz is the owner and sole engineer at Ghosxt, a Salinas, CA managed IT and cybersecurity provider. He holds an M.S. in Cybersecurity and Information Assurance (WGU, 2026) and nine industry certifications including CompTIA SecurityX, CySA+, and Microsoft AZ-104, with prior DoD and federal contractor infrastructure experience. More about Ulises →

TL;DR: October is Cybersecurity Awareness Month. Most small businesses mark it with a forwarded email and nothing else. Four things are worth doing while it's on the calendar: run one real training session, confirm phishing-resistant MFA is on everywhere, verify patching and backups actually ran, and make sure everyone knows who to call first.

Four things worth doing this October

  1. Run one real training session, not a forwarded email or a ten-minute video nobody remembers by November.
  2. Confirm phishing-resistant MFA is on for every account that touches email, money, or client data, including the accounts that predate MFA becoming standard.
  3. Verify patching and backups actually ran instead of assuming a system in the background is handling it.
  4. Make sure every employee knows who to call first the moment something looks wrong, before they're the one deciding under pressure.

None of these require a big budget or a new project. They require picking a date on the calendar and actually doing them.

Cybersecurity Awareness Month comes around every October, and for a lot of small businesses it means exactly one thing: a vendor sends an email with a poster attached, someone forwards it to the team, and nothing else happens. That's a missed opportunity. The month is a built-in, calendar-anchored excuse to do a short list of things that provide real value and are otherwise easy to keep pushing to "next quarter." Below is that list, without the trivia quiz.

1. Replace the slideshow with one real training session

A forwarded email or an annual click-through video satisfies a checkbox and does little else. What actually changes behavior is a short, live session where employees see what a real phishing attempt looks like, ask questions, and hear about the specific tactics showing up in their inbox that month. Pair it with ongoing security awareness training and periodic phishing simulations rather than treating October as the whole program. Thirty minutes once a year is better than nothing. Thirty minutes plus quarterly follow-ups is what actually reduces how often someone clicks.

2. Turn on phishing-resistant MFA everywhere it isn't already

The accounts most likely to be missing multi-factor authentication are the ones nobody has touched since they were set up: a shared mailbox, an old vendor login, an account created before MFA rollout became routine. October is a good excuse to pull the full list of accounts with access to email, financial systems, or client data and check each one, rather than assuming the rollout from a year or two ago covered everything. For Microsoft 365 environments this means confirming Conditional Access policies actually apply to every user, not just the ones added after the policy was written. For Google Workspace, it means confirming phishing-resistant MFA is enforced at the identity provider level, not left optional.

3. Confirm patching and backups actually ran

Most small businesses believe their systems are being patched and backed up because someone told them that's how it's supposed to work. October is a reasonable time to actually verify it: ask for a patch compliance report showing which devices are current and which aren't, and confirm the last successful cloud backup of Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace data, with a real date attached. A backup job that silently stopped running three months ago is worse than no backup plan at all, because nobody finds out until it's needed.

Where to start this week

Pick one item from the list above and put a date on the calendar this week, the same way you'd schedule any other maintenance. Don't try to do all four at once if the business hasn't done any of them recently. Start with confirming MFA coverage, since a single account without it is usually how an intrusion starts, then work through the rest before the month is over. Awareness Month works best as an annual forcing function for a handful of specific checks, not as a program in itself.

Frequently asked questions

What is Cybersecurity Awareness Month?

It runs every October and is a recurring, calendar-anchored reminder for organizations to review their security practices. For most small businesses it arrives as a vendor email and gets forgotten, but the timing itself is useful as a built-in excuse to do a handful of things that are otherwise easy to keep putting off.

Does a small business actually need to do anything for it?

There's no requirement to participate, but the month is a convenient trigger for four things worth doing anyway: a real training session, confirming phishing-resistant MFA is on everywhere, verifying patching and backups are actually running, and making sure every employee knows who to call first.

What's the single highest-value thing to do in October?

Confirming that phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication is turned on for every account that can access email, financial systems, or client data, including accounts set up before MFA became standard. A single account without it is usually how an intrusion starts.

Is a once-a-year training session enough?

No. A single annual session is better than nothing, but attackers change tactics faster than an annual cadence keeps up with. Awareness training works best as an ongoing habit, ideally with regular phishing simulations, with October serving as the anchor point rather than the entire program.
