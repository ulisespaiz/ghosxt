Email Bombing: Why You Are Suddenly Getting Hundreds of Spam Emails

Ulises Paiz, Owner, Ghosxt

TL;DR: A sudden flood of spam is usually cover for something else: a fraudulent charge, a password change, or a fake IT support call. Check your money and your logins before you clean the inbox.

Five steps when your inbox floods.

  1. Do not mass delete. The email that matters is buried in the pile.
  2. Search for money. Look for your bank, cards, payment apps and stores.
  3. Check your accounts. Log in directly and review recent activity.
  4. Lock the mailbox. Change the password and confirm MFA is on.
  5. Call IT yourself. Do not trust anyone who contacts you offering to fix it.

If you run a small business in Monterey County or anywhere on the Central Coast and an inbox suddenly fills with hundreds of sign-up confirmations and newsletters, treat it as an alarm, not a nuisance. This is called email bombing, and it is rarely about the spam. It is cover for something the attacker does not want you to see.

This guide covers what an email bomb hides, what to do in the first hour, and how to make your business a harder target.

1. Why you are suddenly getting hundreds of spam emails

In an email bombing attack, also called subscription bombing, someone feeds your address into hundreds of website sign-up forms at once. Each site sends a real welcome or confirmation message, so the flood passes most spam filters.

  • It buries a real alert. Somewhere in the pile is a message the attacker wants you to miss: a purchase receipt, a new payee notice, a password reset, or a bank transfer confirmation.
  • It sets up a fake help desk. Minutes after the flood starts, "IT support" calls or messages and offers to fix it. That caller is the attacker, and the fix is remote access to your computer. We covered this play in our post on help desk impersonation on Microsoft Teams.
  • It needs nothing from you. The attacker only needs your email address. A flood does not prove your mailbox was hacked, but it often means another account tied to that address is being used right now.

2. What to do in the first hour

The instinct is to select all and delete. Resist it until you have found what the flood is hiding.

  • Search before you clean. Search the inbox, junk and trash for your bank, card issuers, payment apps, payroll provider and online stores. Look for words like "order," "receipt," "transfer," "new device" and "password."
  • Log in the direct way. Open your bank and vendor accounts by typing the address or using the app, not a link in an email. Review recent charges, payees and contact details.
  • Call the bank if anything is off. Speed matters with fraudulent transfers. Use the number on your card or statement.
  • Secure the mailbox. Change the email password, confirm multi-factor authentication is on, and check for a hidden forwarding rule.
  • Skip the unsubscribe links. Clicking through hundreds of messages wastes the hour that counts. Filter them later.
  • Refuse surprise help. If someone contacts you about the spam, hang up and call your IT provider on a number you already have. See our guide to help desk social engineering.

3. How to make your business a harder target

You cannot stop a stranger from typing your address into a web form. You can limit what the flood is able to hide and how far the follow-up call gets.

  • Turn on text or app alerts for money. Set your bank and card accounts to alert by text or app as well as email, so one buried message is not your only warning.
  • Tighten the mail filter. Microsoft 365 and Google Workspace both let an admin raise bulk mail filtering and add temporary rules during a flood. Your IT provider can do this in minutes.
  • Teach one rule. IT never calls out of the blue to fix a problem you did not report. Staff should report a flood, not accept help for it. Our post on reporting suspicious emails shows how to build the habit.
  • Limit remote access. Staff should not be able to hand control of a work computer to an outside caller. Removing local admin rights closes most of that door.

The takeaway

An email bomb is a smoke screen. Find the buried alert, check your money, lock the mailbox, and treat any unrequested "support" contact as part of the attack. Frequently asked questions

Why am I suddenly getting hundreds of spam emails?

A sudden flood of sign-up confirmations and newsletters is usually an email bombing attack. Someone entered your address into hundreds of web forms at once, often to bury a real alert about a purchase, a password change, or a bank transfer.

What is email bombing?

Email bombing, also called subscription bombing, is when an attacker floods one inbox with hundreds or thousands of messages in a short time. The goal is to hide important emails or to set up a fake IT support call.

Should I delete all the emails or click unsubscribe?

Not yet. First search the flood for messages from your bank, payment apps, online stores, and password resets. Do not click unsubscribe on hundreds of messages. Filter them out after you have checked your accounts.

Does an email bomb mean my email was hacked?

Not always. The attacker only needs your address to start a flood. It does often mean another account tied to that address, such as a bank, card, or shopping account, is being used right now, so check those first.

How do I stop an email bombing attack?

Check your financial accounts, change the email password, confirm multi-factor authentication is on, and tell your IT provider so they can tighten the spam filter. Most floods fade within a day or two.
