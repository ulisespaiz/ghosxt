Malicious Inbox Forwarding Rules: The Hidden Sign of Email Compromise (2026)

Ulises Paiz, Owner, Ghosxt

Ulises Paiz is the owner and sole engineer at Ghosxt, a Salinas, CA managed IT and cybersecurity provider. He holds an M.S. in Cybersecurity and Information Assurance (WGU, 2026) and nine industry certifications including CompTIA SecurityX, CySA+, and Microsoft AZ-104, with prior DoD and federal contractor infrastructure experience. More about Ulises →

TL;DR: A successful phishing attack often leaves no visible trace except one thing: a forwarding rule quietly added to the mailbox. It costs nothing to set up, keeps working after a password reset, and can sit unnoticed for weeks while an attacker waits for an invoice or a wire transfer worth intercepting.

Three things worth knowing about this technique.

  1. It replaces repeated logins with a standing copy of every message that matches a rule the attacker set once and never has to touch again.
  2. It is checkable in minutes, in both Microsoft 365 and Google Workspace, without any special tooling.
  3. It usually means a wider compromise already happened, so finding one is the start of a cleanup, not the end of one.

Most small businesses have never looked at their own mailbox rules. That is exactly why this technique still works.

Ask a small business owner what a successful phishing attack looks like and they will usually describe a moment: a fake invoice, a wired payment, a locked-up server. The quieter version looks like nothing at all. An employee's password gets phished, the attacker signs in once, and instead of doing anything visible they add a single forwarding rule to the mailbox and log back out. Nothing about the account looks different. The employee keeps working normally. For weeks afterward, every email that mentions an invoice, a wire transfer, or a vendor payment gets a silent copy sent somewhere else, and nobody notices until money moves to the wrong account.

1. Why a forwarding rule is the attacker's tool of choice

A forwarding rule solves two problems for an attacker at once. First, it survives a password reset. Once the rule exists inside the mailbox itself, changing the password does nothing to remove it, so the attacker keeps receiving copies of new mail even after the "fix" has already happened. Second, it removes the need to log back in. Every additional sign-in carries a small risk of tripping an alert or catching a user's attention if they happen to check recent activity. A rule set once does the watching for them, silently, in the background, for as long as it exists.

2. How to check your own mailbox for one

In Microsoft 365, open the mail settings for the account and review every entry under Rules for anything that forwards, redirects, or automatically deletes messages, especially rules with generic names or none at all. An administrator does not need to check every employee one at a time; the admin center can run a report across every mailbox in the tenant in one pass, which is worth doing periodically rather than only after something feels wrong. In Google Workspace, the check is similar: open Gmail settings and look under both Filters and Blocked Addresses and Forwarding and POP/IMAP for any forwarding address the team did not set up, and a Workspace administrator can review the same setting across every account from the admin console.

3. Closing the door it came through

Finding a bad rule fixes the symptom. Closing the door it came through is the part that keeps it from happening again. Phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication removes most of the paths that let an attacker capture a working session in the first place, and blocking legacy authentication protocols closes off one of the easier bypass routes for stolen credentials. Beyond that, treating a mailbox rule audit as a routine maintenance task, not an incident response step, catches this technique regardless of how the initial compromise happened. In a managed environment, an unexpected new forwarding rule is exactly the kind of quiet change that ongoing monitoring is built to flag before it turns into a wire transfer loss.

A five-minute check worth doing this week

Most businesses only look at mailbox rules after an incident has already made the loss obvious. Checking now, before there is a reason to suspect anything, costs almost nothing and takes less time than reading this post took. Pair it with the habits covered in our business email compromise prevention guide and the identity-hardening checklist for Microsoft 365, and the same account takeover that a forwarding rule depends on becomes much harder to pull off in the first place.

Frequently asked questions

How do I check for a hidden forwarding rule in Outlook or Gmail?

In Outlook or Microsoft 365, open mail settings and review every rule under Rules, looking for anything that forwards, redirects, or auto-deletes messages. An administrator can also check every mailbox at once through the admin center rather than relying on each employee to look. In Gmail, check Settings under both Filters and Blocked Addresses and Forwarding and POP/IMAP for a forwarding address nobody on the team added.

Why do attackers add a forwarding rule instead of just reading email directly?

A forwarding rule keeps working even after a password reset closes the door the attacker first walked through, and it lets them monitor a mailbox quietly in the background while they wait for an invoice or a wire transfer conversation worth intercepting, instead of logging in repeatedly and risking an alert.

Can MFA stop someone from creating a forwarding rule?

Multi-factor authentication makes the initial account takeover much harder, but an attacker who has already gotten past it, through a phishing kit that steals an active session or a push-bombing attack, can create a rule the same as the legitimate user. Phishing-resistant MFA closes most of those bypass paths, but reviewing mailbox rules on a regular basis still matters as a second layer.

What should I do if I find a forwarding rule I did not create?

Delete the rule, reset the account password, and sign it out of every active session, then review sent mail and any available sign-in history for the window the rule was active. Treat the rule as evidence that a wider compromise already happened rather than as the incident itself.
