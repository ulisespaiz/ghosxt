Fake Voicemail Notification Emails: How to Spot Voicemail Phishing

Ulises Paiz, Owner, Ghosxt

Ulises Paiz is the owner and sole engineer at Ghosxt, a Salinas, CA managed IT and cybersecurity provider. He holds an M.S. in Cybersecurity and Information Assurance (WGU, 2026) and nine industry certifications including CompTIA SecurityX, CySA+, and Microsoft AZ-104, with prior DoD and federal contractor infrastructure experience. More about Ulises →

TL;DR: An email that says “You have a new voicemail” is one of the most common ways attackers steal Microsoft 365 and Google Workspace passwords. A real voicemail email carries the recording as an audio file you can play. A fake one sends you to a web page, an HTML file or a QR code and asks you to sign in to listen. If it asks for your password, it is not a voicemail.

Four checks that catch a fake voicemail email.

  1. Real voicemail plays without a sign-in. Any login screen between you and the message is the scam.
  2. Look at the attachment. An audio file is normal. An .htm, .html, .svg, a PDF with a “Play” button, or a QR code is not.
  3. Check where your voicemail really lives. Open your phone app or Teams directly instead of the email.
  4. Report it, then delete it. One report protects everyone else who got the same email.

A fake voicemail notification email is a phishing message dressed up as an alert from your phone system, saying a caller left you a message. This guide is for small business owners, office managers and staff in Monterey County and surrounding areas who get voicemail by email. It shows what a real voicemail email looks like, the signs of a fake one, and what to do if someone already clicked.

The blind spot: most teams are trained to distrust emails about money and passwords. A missed call feels like neither. It feels like a customer trying to reach you, and that is exactly why it works.

1. How the fake voicemail email works

The subject line reads something like “New voicemail from (831) 555-0142, 0:47” or “Missed call: 1 new message.” The body copies the look of a phone system notice, often with a Microsoft or phone-company logo, a caller number and a message length.

  • The “recording” is a trap. It is a link, an HTML or SVG attachment, a PDF with a play button, or a QR code. See our guide to risky email attachment types for why a saved web page is dangerous.
  • It lands on a fake sign-in page. The page looks like Microsoft 365 or Google, and your email address is often already filled in, which makes it feel real.
  • Some pages steal more than the password. Newer kits sit between you and the real sign-in, so they also catch the code from your authenticator app or text. Our guide to session hijacking explains how that works.

The QR code version moves the attack to your phone, where links are harder to inspect. Our QR code phishing guide covers that variation.

2. What a real voicemail email looks like

If your business uses Microsoft Teams for phones, Microsoft’s own documentation says voicemail arrives in your Outlook mailbox as a standard email from noreply@skype.voicemail.microsoft.com, with the recording attached as an audio file and a written transcript if transcription is turned on. The same message also shows up under Calls in Teams. Most other business phone systems that send voicemail to email work the same way: the audio is attached, and you just press play.

Two points matter more than the sender name, because a display name can be faked:

  • Real voicemail never asks you to sign in to listen. You are already signed in to your mailbox.
  • If your company does not get voicemail by email at all, every voicemail email is fake. Ask your IT provider how your phones deliver messages, and tell staff the answer.

3. Red flags in a voicemail notification

Any one of these is reason enough not to open it. Two together almost always mean phishing.

  • A sign-in page after you click “Play.” This is the one that matters most.
  • An attachment that is not audio. File names like VM_Message.htm, Voice_Note.svg or Play_Audio.pdf.
  • An outside sender. Your external sender warning banner should flag any “voicemail” that did not come from your own phone system.
  • Urgency that real voicemail does not have. “Message expires in 24 hours” or “Your mailbox will be deleted.”
  • A message that only exists in the email. Open your phone app or Teams. If the voicemail is not there, it was never real.

Phone scams also run the other way: a real voicemail asking you to call back about an invoice or account problem. Our vishing guide covers how to handle those.

4. If someone already clicked and signed in

Move fast. A stolen work password is usually used within minutes, often to send more phishing from your real mailbox or to set up an invoice scam.

  • Change the password from a clean device, such as a phone on cellular data, and sign out of all sessions.
  • Check sign-in methods and inbox rules. Remove any authenticator, phone number or hidden forwarding rule you did not add.
  • Call your IT provider. A phone call beats an email if the mailbox may be in someone else’s hands.
  • Warn coworkers. The same email almost always went to more than one person.

Our step-by-step guide to what to do after clicking a phishing link walks through the full first hour.

5. Settings that stop most of these emails

  • Block HTML and SVG attachments in your email security settings if your business has no need to receive them. Real voicemail never uses them.
  • Give staff a one-click report button and thank people who use it. Our guide to the phishing report button shows how.
  • Move to phishing-resistant sign-in. Passkeys do not work on a fake page, so a stolen password alone gets an attacker nowhere.
  • Tell staff how your real voicemail arrives. One sentence in onboarding removes most of the guesswork.

The takeaway

A voicemail you have to sign in to hear is not a voicemail. Teach that one rule, check the phone app instead of the email, and block the attachment types real voicemail never uses. Add phishing-resistant sign-in and a fast way to report, and this scam loses most of its power. Frequently asked questions

Is a new voicemail email real or a scam?

A real voicemail email carries the recording as an audio file you can play, and it never asks you to sign in. If the email wants you to open a web page, an HTML or SVG file, a PDF button or a QR code and then enter your password to listen, it is phishing.

What does a real Microsoft Teams voicemail email look like?

Microsoft says Teams voicemail arrives in your Outlook mailbox from noreply@skype.voicemail.microsoft.com, with the recording attached as an audio file and a transcript if transcription is turned on. The same message also appears under Calls in Teams.

What should I do if I entered my password on a fake voicemail page?

Change the password from a clean device, sign out of all sessions, check your sign-in methods and inbox rules for anything you did not add, and call your IT provider right away. Treat the account as compromised until it has been checked.

Why do scammers use fake voicemail emails?

Voicemail feels urgent and personal, and many people get real voicemail by email, so a fake notice looks routine. The goal is almost always your Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace password, which opens your email, files and everything linked to them.

How can a small business block fake voicemail emails?

Block HTML and SVG attachments if you do not need them, keep an external sender warning on, give staff a one-click report button, and move to phishing-resistant sign-in such as passkeys so a stolen password alone is not enough.
