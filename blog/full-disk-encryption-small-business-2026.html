Full-Disk Encryption for Small Business: The BitLocker and FileVault Rollout Guide

Ulises Paiz, Founder, Ghosxt

Ulises Paiz, Founder of Ghosxt, has 10+ years in IT infrastructure and cybersecurity, deep DoD infrastructure experience, and 9 certifications including CySA+, Security+, and AZ-104. Before founding Ghosxt, he served as a Senior Solutions Consultant for the DoD and built security programs for 40+ Central Coast businesses. More about Ulises →

TL;DR: one setting turns a lost laptop into a non-event

A laptop left in a car, a coffee shop, or a rideshare is one of the most common ways small business data goes missing. Without full-disk encryption, whoever finds it can pull the drive and read every file on it. With it turned on, the data is unreadable without the key. It's a free setting most fleets still don't have on everywhere.

  • Windows needs Pro or Enterprise, not Home. BitLocker isn't available on Windows Home, and a lot of small business laptops still ship with it.
  • Escrow the recovery key before you enable anything. Skip this step and a forgotten password becomes permanent data loss instead of a five-minute admin lookup.
  • Insurers are starting to ask. Encryption is showing up as a checkbox on cyber insurance applications and renewals, and "no" can mean a higher premium or a declined policy.

This pairs well with our MDM post (the management layer that makes recovery-key escrow possible) and our cyber insurance renewal checklist (where encryption now shows up as a line item).

Most of the cybersecurity conversation with small business owners is about stopping someone from breaking in remotely: phishing, MFA, patching. Full-disk encryption solves a quieter, more physical problem: what happens when a device with company data on it simply leaves the building without permission, whether that's theft, a lost bag, or a laptop that gets sold or recycled without being wiped first. It's one of the few controls that's already built into every modern operating system, costs nothing extra, and takes minutes to turn on, yet it's still missing on a large share of small business fleets.

What full-disk encryption actually protects against

Full-disk encryption scrambles everything on a drive so it's unreadable without the correct key, which is normally unlocked automatically by a logged-in, authorized user and invisible day to day. The scenario it defends against is specific: someone with physical possession of the device trying to read the data directly, by pulling the drive and connecting it to another machine, which completely bypasses a Windows or Mac login password. A login password stops a casual snoop; it does nothing against someone who removes the drive. Encryption is the control that closes that gap, and it's exactly the scenario behind a lost laptop, a stolen delivery vehicle with a mounted tablet, or old hardware that gets donated or resold with data still on it.

BitLocker, FileVault, and the edition trap

Windows has shipped BitLocker for over a decade, and every current Mac ships with FileVault, so for most businesses this isn't a purchase, it's a setting. The catch on the Windows side is licensing: BitLocker requires Windows 10 or 11 Pro, Enterprise, or Education. Windows Home only gets the lighter Device Encryption feature, which activates automatically on limited hardware and ties recovery to a personal Microsoft account rather than one the business controls. A fair number of small business laptops, especially ones bought retail instead of through a business channel, are quietly running Home edition with no real encryption option at all, which is worth auditing before assuming the fleet is covered.

The step that actually matters: escrowing the key

Turning encryption on is the easy part. The step that prevents it from becoming a support disaster is escrowing the recovery key somewhere the business controls before rollout, not after the first forgotten password. For Windows fleets, that means Microsoft Entra ID or Intune; for Macs, Apple Business Manager or a tool like Jamf. Done this way, a forgotten password or a failed login after a hardware repair is a five-minute admin lookup. Skipped, it's a permanently locked drive with no way back in, which is the one scenario that turns encryption from a safety net into a liability. Roll it out in a batch, confirm every key escrowed successfully, and only then push it fleet-wide.

FAQs about full-disk encryption for small business

Does Windows Home come with BitLocker?

No. BitLocker requires Windows 10/11 Pro, Enterprise, or Education. Windows Home ships with a more limited feature called Device Encryption, which only activates automatically on certain modern hardware and ties recovery to a personal Microsoft account rather than a business-managed one, which is a poor fit for a company fleet.

What happens if an employee forgets their BitLocker or FileVault password?

As long as the recovery key was escrowed somewhere the business controls, such as Microsoft Entra ID, Intune, or Jamf, an admin can look it up and unlock the drive without any data loss. This is exactly why escrowing recovery keys before rollout matters; without it, a forgotten password can mean the data on that drive is gone for good.

Does encrypting a laptop slow it down?

On any machine built in the last several years, no. Both BitLocker and FileVault use hardware-accelerated AES encryption built into modern CPUs, and the performance difference is not noticeable in day-to-day use. The exception is old hardware without that acceleration, which is one more reason overdue replacements should get folded into the rollout instead of encrypted as-is.
