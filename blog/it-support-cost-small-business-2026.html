How Much Should a Small Business Pay for IT Support in 2026?

Ulises Paiz, Founder, Ghosxt

Ulises Paiz, Founder of Ghosxt, has 10+ years in IT infrastructure and cybersecurity, deep DoD infrastructure experience, and 9 certifications including CySA+, Security+, and AZ-104. Before founding Ghosxt, he served as a Senior Solutions Consultant for the DoD and built security programs for 40+ Central Coast businesses. More about Ulises →

TL;DR: the pricing model matters more than the rate

IT support gets priced five different ways, and each one pays your provider for a different outcome. Break/fix earns more when things break. Managed services earns more when they do not. Before you compare monthly numbers, work out which model you are actually buying, because the same dollar figure buys very different behavior depending on the answer.

  • The five models: break/fix hourly, prepaid block hours, managed services priced per user or per device, co-managed IT alongside internal staff, and project work billed separately from all of the above.
  • A concrete reference point: Ghosxt publishes its rates, so this post uses them. $600 a month flat for teams of 1 to 4 users, then $125, $175, or $250 per user per month depending on the depth of the security and compliance stack. Those are our published rates, not an industry survey.
  • What moves the number: security stack depth, compliance obligations, onsite needs, how many servers and how much infrastructure you run, and whether you need coverage outside business hours.

For the Salinas version with local market context, see our managed IT cost in Salinas post. If the terminology is still fuzzy, start with IT support vs. managed IT services.

"How much should we be paying for IT?" is a hard question to answer honestly, because the number on its own means almost nothing. A business paying $500 a month and a business paying $5,000 a month can both be getting a fair deal, or both be getting taken, depending on headcount, what is included, and what happens when something breaks at 6pm on a Friday.

What follows is the structure underneath the number: how IT support gets priced, what each model rewards your provider for doing, what drives the monthly figure up or down, and the questions that separate a real quote from a marketing number. Every dollar figure below is one of Ghosxt's published rates, labeled as such.

The five ways IT support gets priced

1. Break/fix hourly

You call when something is broken and pay by the hour, plus parts. No contract, no monthly bill, and no work happening while you are not looking. It suits a very small operation with a handful of computers, no servers, no compliance obligations, and enough tolerance to lose a day.

The incentive is the part worth understanding. Under break/fix, your provider's revenue comes from your problems. Nobody is patching, monitoring, testing backups, or hardening accounts, because none of that is billable, and an hour spent preventing a failure is an hour of revenue that does not happen. That is not a claim about anyone's ethics. It is just how the contract is built. Our break/fix vs. managed IT page walks through the comparison in more detail.

2. Prepaid block hours

You buy a block of hours up front, usually at a discount off the standard rate, and draw against it. This is still break/fix, with a better rate and a slightly worse dynamic: blocks often expire, and the pressure to use hours before they do tends to produce work that is not the work you needed most. Blocks make sense for funding a known chunk of project work. They are not a substitute for somebody maintaining the environment.

3. Managed services, per user or per device

A flat recurring fee covers monitoring, patching, endpoint security, backups, helpdesk, and administration of your Microsoft 365 or Google environment. Per-user pricing counts people. Per-device pricing counts endpoints.

Per user dominates the small business market because it is predictable and it does not punish an employee for having a laptop, a desktop, and a phone. Per device can look cheaper on the headline rate and gets expensive once you count everything with an address on the network. When you compare the two, do the arithmetic against your real device inventory, not your headcount.

The incentive flips under this model. When the fee is fixed, every ticket costs your provider money, so preventing tickets is how they stay profitable. Their interest and yours point the same direction, which is the real argument for managed services, more than any feature list. If you are working out whether you have outgrown hourly support, our post on the signs a small business needs managed IT covers the tells.

4. Co-managed IT

You have internal IT, one person or a small team, and a provider covers what that team cannot: after-hours coverage, the security stack, escalation for problems outside their depth, project capacity, and vacation coverage. Pricing is usually per user at a reduced rate, or a defined scope for a fixed fee, since the provider is not carrying the whole load.

This fits businesses around 50 users and up, where a full-time internal person is justified but a full internal team is not. It is also the honest answer for companies whose internal tech is capable and buried. Our co-managed IT page covers how the split usually gets drawn.

5. Project work

Server replacements, Microsoft 365 migrations, network builds, office moves, and security remediation get quoted separately in every model above. Fixed-fee scoping favors you, because the provider absorbs the risk of a bad estimate. Time and materials favors the provider. Either can be fair, as long as you know which one you signed.

The thing to watch: if a provider sells you managed services and then bills every change as a project, you are paying a retainer for break/fix. The line between included support and billable project belongs in writing before you sign, not in a negotiation afterward.

What managed IT actually costs

Most providers will not publish a number, which is why this question is so hard to research. Ghosxt does, so here are ours as a concrete reference point. These are our published rates, not an industry survey or a market average:

  • Tiny Team Managed Security: $600 per month flat, for teams of 1 to 4 users.
  • Core Managed IT: $125 per user per month. Devices patched, monitored, and supported, with MFA and security fundamentals built in.
  • Secure Growth: $175 per user per month. Adds advanced email filtering, dark web and DNS filtering, password management, and Microsoft 365 and OneDrive cloud backup.
  • Compliance & Continuity: $250 per user per month. Adds managed SIEM, identity threat detection, vCIO and quarterly reviews, compliance support, and compliance-grade backup retention.

The full contents of each tier are on our pricing page. Whichever provider you are talking to, the same five variables move a per-user price up or down:

  • Security stack depth. Antivirus and patching is the floor. EDR, MFA enforcement, email filtering, DNS filtering, and awareness training is the middle. Managed SIEM, identity threat detection, and monitored response is the top, and it is the single biggest driver of the spread between tiers.
  • Compliance requirements. HIPAA, CMMC, PCI, cyber insurance attestations, and customer security questionnaires each add documentation, retention, and evidence work that has to be priced in.
  • Onsite needs. A cloud-first business running laptops needs far fewer visits than a pack house, a clinic, or a manufacturing floor full of equipment that cannot be touched remotely.
  • Servers and infrastructure count. On-premises servers, hypervisors, line-of-business applications, and specialty equipment each carry maintenance a purely Microsoft 365 environment does not.
  • Coverage hours. Business-hours support and around-the-clock support are different products. If you run a night shift or ship before dawn, that belongs in the contract rather than assumed.

Three illustrative profiles

These are illustrative examples, not actual customers. They exist to show how the model and the number connect, and every figure is an example calculation using Ghosxt's published rates.

A 5-user professional office

An accounting, legal, or design office: five people, laptops, everything in Microsoft 365, one shared file location, no servers. Managed services priced per user fits cleanly, so the judgment call is tier, not model. As example calculations at our published rates, five users on Core Managed IT at $125 comes to $625 per month, and five users on Secure Growth at $175 comes to $875 per month.

What decides between them is data sensitivity, not headcount. An office holding client financial records, legal files, or health information is a wire-fraud target regardless of size, and the email filtering, DNS filtering, and password management in the higher tier target exactly that. Below five users the per-user math stops working for most providers, which is why flat-rate tiers exist. Ours is $600 per month for one to four people.

A 25-user agriculture company with a pack house

Twenty-five users split across an office, a pack house, and a cooler, with shared workstations on the floor, scanners, an ERP or traceability system, seasonal staff, and equipment that cannot go offline mid-season. Per user again, at a higher tier, with project work quoted alongside it. Twenty-five users on Secure Growth at $175 is $4,375 per month as an example calculation.

Three things push an operation like this toward the higher tier: seasonal account churn, the fact that downtime during harvest costs shipping windows rather than convenience, and buyer or audit requirements that ask hard questions about how records are protected. Shared floor stations also change the shape of the work, since per-user pricing counts people while a pack house runs stations, so settle how shared devices get counted before you sign.

A 75-user multi-site business

Seventy-five users across two or three locations, most likely with one or two internal IT people already. This is where co-managed usually beats fully outsourced. Seventy-five users on Secure Growth at $175 would be $13,125 per month as a straight example calculation, but that figure assumes a provider carrying the entire load, and at this size you are usually not buying that. You are buying the parts an internal team cannot cover alone: the security stack, after-hours and escalation coverage, project capacity, and someone senior in the room for planning.

Priced that way, co-managed engagements get scoped rather than counted off headcount, so the honest answer at this size is that the number depends on where the line falls between internal and external. Draw that line in writing before anyone prices it.

The cost nobody itemizes

The monthly invoice is the visible half of what IT costs. The other half is what happens when things do not work, and it never appears as a line item.

Idle payroll during an outage is the clearest example. When a system a team depends on goes down, you are still paying everyone in the building and none of that time produces anything. A business that ships on a schedule carries a second cost on top of it, because a missed shipping window does not just delay revenue, it can cost the order outright.

Reactive support carries its own premium. Our published rate card charges $175 an hour for scheduled engineering work, $250 an hour after hours, and $350 an hour for emergency incident response. That gap is not arbitrary and it is not unique to us. Unplanned work costs more everywhere, because it interrupts everything else and it lands on the worst possible timeline.

Deferred maintenance compounds quietly. A server left unpatched, a firewall past end of support, a backup nobody has ever restored from, a workstation running an operating system that stopped getting updates: each one saves a little this quarter and bills for it later, either as an emergency replacement at the worst time or as the way an incident gets in. The reason managed services can end up cheaper than hourly is not that hourly rates are high. It is that the maintenance actually gets done, so the expensive events happen less often.

None of this argues that every business should buy the top tier. It argues that comparing a managed quote against your current spending is the wrong comparison. The right one is your current spending plus what your last bad week cost you.

Questions to ask any provider before you sign

  • What are your response expectations, in writing? Not "fast" or "same day." Ask for target response times by severity and what happens when one is missed. If it is not in the agreement, it is a sales sentence.
  • What is included versus billable? Get specifics: user onboarding and offboarding, new machine setup, Microsoft 365 changes, printer problems, vendor coordination, after-hours calls, onsite visits, travel. This is where surprise invoices come from.
  • What exactly is in the security stack? Product names, not categories. "Endpoint protection" can mean built-in antivirus or a monitored EDR platform with a response team behind it, and that difference is most of the price.
  • What happens at offboarding, and who owns the data? Ask how you get your documentation, credentials, backups, and configurations if you leave, how long it takes, and what it costs. A provider who will not answer that cleanly is telling you something.
  • How long is the contract, and what is the exit? Term length, notice period, and any penalty. A longer term in exchange for a better rate can be fine. A longer term with no exit for non-performance is not.

FAQs about IT support cost for small business

How much should a small business expect to pay for IT support?

It depends far more on the pricing model and the scope than on the size of the business, which is why a single number is not useful on its own. As a concrete reference point, Ghosxt's published managed IT rates are $125, $175, or $250 per user per month depending on the depth of the security and compliance stack, with a $600 per month flat tier for teams of one to four users. Those are our published rates rather than an industry average. When comparing providers, compare what is included at each price, since the same monthly figure can buy a full managed stack at one provider and monitoring alone at another.

Is per-user or per-device pricing better for a small business?

Per user is usually better for the customer and it has become the dominant model for small business managed services. It counts people instead of hardware, so an employee with a laptop, a desktop, and a phone is billed once, and the bill stays predictable when someone picks up a second device. Per-device pricing can advertise a lower headline rate and then grow as you count servers, laptops, tablets, and anything else with an address on the network. If you are weighing a per-device quote against a per-user quote, run the per-device math against your real inventory before deciding which one is cheaper.

Is managed IT worth it compared with paying hourly only when something breaks?

It depends on how much downtime costs you. Hourly break/fix can be reasonable for a very small business with no servers, no compliance obligations, and the ability to absorb a day of being down. Past that point the arithmetic usually favors managed services, not because hourly rates are unfair but because nobody does the preventive work under an hourly arrangement. Patching, backup testing, monitoring, and identity hardening are not billable events, so they do not happen, and you end up paying an emergency rate for problems maintenance would have prevented.
