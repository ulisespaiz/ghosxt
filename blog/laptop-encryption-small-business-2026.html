Laptop and Device Encryption for Small Business: The Cheapest Insurance Policy You're Not Using

Ulises Paiz, Founder, Ghosxt

Ulises Paiz, Founder of Ghosxt, has 10+ years in IT infrastructure and cybersecurity, deep DoD infrastructure experience, and 9 certifications including CySA+, Security+, and AZ-104. Before founding Ghosxt, he served as a Senior Solutions Consultant for the DoD and built security programs for 40+ Central Coast businesses. More about Ulises →

TL;DR: it's already on the machine, just not turned on

Every business laptop sold in the last decade ships with full-disk encryption built in, BitLocker on Windows, FileVault on a Mac, at no extra cost. Most small businesses have never verified it's actually enabled. That single setting is the difference between a lost laptop being a $1,200 hardware replacement and a reportable data breach.

  • It's free and it's already installed. No software to buy, no license to add, just a setting that's off far more often than owners assume.
  • It has a legal upside, not just a technical one. California's breach notification law is triggered by unencrypted personal information, encryption can take a lost device out of that trigger entirely.
  • The failure mode isn't the encryption, it's the recovery key. Turned on without an escrowed key, a forgotten password becomes a permanently bricked laptop.

Read this alongside our California data breach notification law post (the legal reason this matters), the business travel security post (why devices leaving the office change the math), and the MDM post (how to enforce this across a whole fleet instead of device by device).

Ask a small business owner if their laptops are encrypted and the honest answer is usually "I think so." That guess is the entire problem. Full-disk encryption isn't a product you buy, it's a setting that's been sitting inside Windows and macOS for years, free, and it's routinely left off on new machines because nobody's job is to check. The gap between "probably encrypted" and "verified encrypted" is exactly the gap that turns a lost laptop from an insurance claim into a legal incident, a client notification letter, and a very uncomfortable phone call.

What full-disk encryption actually protects, and what it doesn't

Full-disk encryption scrambles everything written to a device's storage so it's unreadable without the correct key. On a properly configured business laptop, that key is released automatically and invisibly when the machine boots and the user logs in, so nobody notices it's running day to day. The protection only matters the moment the device leaves an authorized user's hands: dropped in a hotel room in Carmel, left in a rideshare, or stolen from a parked car outside a job site. Pull the drive out and plug it into another computer, or boot from a USB stick to bypass the login screen, and without encryption every file, email cache, saved password, and cached credential on that disk is just sitting there in the open.

It's worth being precise about the limits, because encryption gets oversold as a cure-all. It protects data at rest on a powered-off or locked device. It does nothing for a laptop that's stolen while unlocked and logged in, it doesn't stop phishing, it doesn't stop malware, and on Windows 11 it can, in specific circumstances, be bypassed by someone with sustained physical access to an unlocked recovery environment, which is exactly what the YellowKey BitLocker bypass we covered demonstrated. None of that is a reason to skip it, a lock on the front door doesn't stop every burglar either, it's a reason to pair encryption with a screen lock, a strong PIN at boot, and the layered controls further down.

Why this matters more than most owners realize

The technical case for encryption is obvious. The part that catches business owners off guard is the legal one. Under California's data breach notification law, the trigger for a mandatory notification isn't "we lost a device," it's unauthorized acquisition of unencrypted personal information. If a laptop containing customer names, employee records, or financial data is lost or stolen and its drive was properly encrypted, with the recovery key never exposed alongside it, that loss generally falls outside the notification requirement entirely. The exact same physical event, a laptop left in a car, produces two completely different outcomes depending on one setting: either a hardware replacement covered by insurance, or a legal obligation to notify every affected person, potentially the state attorney general, and possibly the press.

That gap matters even more for anyone traveling with a device. As we covered in our business travel security post, what determines whether a lost laptop is a $1,200 loss or a walk-in data breach is entirely what was configured before it left the building. Encryption is the single highest-leverage piece of that preparation, because unlike a VPN or a password policy, it protects the data even if every other control failed.

Turning it on: what it costs, and the one thing people skip

For most small businesses, this isn't a purchase, it's a checkbox that's already available. BitLocker ships free with Windows 10 and 11 Pro, Enterprise, and Business editions, which is what most business-purchased laptops run; Windows Home lacks full BitLocker but newer hardware often has a lighter Device Encryption feature on by default. Every Mac sold since 2018 includes FileVault, also free, turned on in System Settings in under a minute. The performance cost is unnoticeable on hardware from the last several years because the encryption runs in a dedicated chip, not the main processor.

The step almost everyone skips is recovery key escrow. Encryption without a saved recovery key means a forgotten password, a failed firmware update, or a swapped motherboard can lock a device permanently, with the data gone for good, not just inaccessible to an attacker. Windows can escrow the key to Microsoft Entra ID or Active Directory automatically; Apple Business Manager does the same for FileVault. Pairing that with a boot-time PIN, the same "TPM plus PIN" setting we recommended in our YellowKey post, closes off the one practical bypass while keeping IT's ability to recover a locked device intact. For a business managing more than a handful of laptops, enforcing all of this automatically, rather than hoping each device was configured correctly by hand, is exactly what mobile device management is built for: one policy, applied to every device, verified on demand instead of assumed.

FAQs about laptop and device encryption for small business

What is full-disk encryption and what does it actually protect against?

Full-disk encryption scrambles everything stored on a device's hard drive so it's unreadable without the correct key, which is normally released automatically at login. It protects data at rest, meaning a laptop that's lost, stolen, or has its drive physically removed. It does not protect a device that's already unlocked and logged in, and it doesn't stop phishing, malware, or an attacker who has the user's password.

Is BitLocker free, and does every version of Windows have it?

BitLocker is included at no extra cost with Windows 10 and 11 Pro, Enterprise, and Business editions, which is what most business-purchased laptops ship with. Windows Home does not include full BitLocker, but newer hardware often ships with a lighter version called Device Encryption enabled automatically. Every modern Mac includes FileVault free as well.

Is a lost laptop still a reportable data breach in California if it was encrypted?

Generally no. California's breach notification law is triggered by unauthorized acquisition of unencrypted personal information. If the laptop's drive was encrypted and the encryption key wasn't also exposed, the loss typically falls outside that legal trigger entirely, turning a reportable incident into an insurance claim for a replacement laptop.

Does encryption slow down a computer or make IT support harder?

On any laptop sold in the last several years, the performance impact is not noticeable in day-to-day use because the encryption and decryption happen automatically in hardware. It doesn't change the login experience at all. The one operational requirement is escrowing the recovery key somewhere IT can retrieve it, without that step a forgotten password or a failed update can lock a device permanently.
