Malicious Email Attachments: Which File Types to Block and How to Handle the Rest

Ulises Paiz, Owner, Ghosxt

Ulises Paiz is the owner and sole engineer at Ghosxt, a Salinas, CA managed IT and cybersecurity provider. He holds an M.S. in Cybersecurity and Information Assurance (WGU, 2026) and nine industry certifications including CompTIA SecurityX, CySA+, and Microsoft AZ-104, with prior DoD and federal contractor infrastructure experience. More about Ulises →

TL;DR: Most email attacks still need one thing: an employee opening a file. Block the file types nobody in your office needs, scan and isolate what remains, and teach a short rule for unexpected attachments. Filters will not catch everything, so the plan has to include the person at the keyboard.

Five steps to take this week.

  1. Block risky file types. Stop program, script, and disk image files at the mail gateway.
  2. Scan and hold archives. Quarantine password-protected ZIP files from outside senders.
  3. Turn off Office macros. Block macros in files that arrive from the internet.
  4. Show file extensions. Make a hidden second extension easy to spot.
  5. Give staff a one-click report. A fast report beats a silent mistake.

If you run a small business in Monterey County or anywhere on the Central Coast, your staff opens attachments all day: invoices, resumes, purchase orders, scanned forms. That routine is exactly what attackers copy.

This guide covers which email attachments are dangerous, why a harmless-looking file can still cause damage, and the controls that cut the risk without slowing the office down.

1. Which file types carry the most risk

Not every attachment is equal. A short list of file types causes a large share of the harm, and most small offices have no reason to receive them by email at all.

  • Programs and scripts. Files ending in .exe, .js, .vbs, .bat, and .scr run code the moment they are opened.
  • Disk images and shortcuts. Files such as .iso and .lnk can launch hidden commands and sometimes slip past basic scanning.
  • Office files with macros. A Word or Excel file that says "enable content to view" is asking you to run code.
  • Archives. A ZIP or similar file can hold any of the above, and a password inside the email can stop a scanner from looking in.
  • HTML attachments. A saved web page can open a fake sign-in form that sends your password to an attacker.

If your team never needs to receive a program file by email, blocking those types costs nothing and removes a whole category of risk.

2. Why "safe" files and familiar senders still hurt

Blocking file types helps, but attackers adapt. Two patterns get around simple rules.

  • The attachment with no malware. A clean-looking PDF may only hold a link or a phone number. Our guide to callback phishing shows how an invoice with no link can still lead to remote access, and QR code phishing hides the link in an image.
  • The trusted sender. A real vendor whose mailbox was taken over can send a real-looking file. See vendor payment fraud and fake e-signature requests for how that plays out.

Writing quality has also improved, so spelling mistakes are no longer a reliable warning. We covered that in AI-written phishing emails.

3. Controls that work without slowing people down

You want a layered setup, so one missed email does not become an incident.

  • Filter at the mail gateway. Both Microsoft 365 and Google Workspace let an admin block file types and quarantine suspicious attachments. Set those rules once and review them quarterly.
  • Authenticate your own domain. SPF, DKIM, and DMARC make it harder to spoof your name to your own customers.
  • Block macros from the internet. Most offices do not need them, and turning them off removes a classic attack path.
  • Keep protection on the laptop. If a file does run, behavior-based endpoint protection can stop it. Fewer rights also help, see local admin rights.
  • Teach one rule. If an attachment is unexpected, confirm with the sender using a phone number you already have. Practice it with phishing simulations.
  • Make reporting easy. If someone does open something odd, our guide on what to do after a click gives the first steps.

The takeaway

Attachments remain a top way in because they work. Block the file types you never need, treat unexpected files as unverified, and keep a safety net on every laptop. For help setting this up, see our cybersecurity and managed IT services, or check current pricing.

Frequently asked questions

Which email attachments are the most dangerous?

Executable and script files such as .exe, .js, .vbs, and .bat are the riskiest, along with disk images like .iso, shortcut files like .lnk, and Office files that ask you to enable macros. Compressed ZIP files are also common because they can hide any of these inside.

Is a PDF attachment safe to open?

Not automatically. A PDF is less risky than a program, but attackers use PDFs that contain a link to a fake login page or a fake invoice with a phone number to call. Treat an unexpected PDF with the same caution as any unexpected link.

Can I get infected just by opening an email?

It is rare for modern mail apps. The usual risk comes from opening an attachment, clicking a link, or enabling macros. Keeping your mail and operating system updated lowers the small remaining risk.

Should a small business block ZIP files?

Blocking all ZIP files is often too disruptive. A better approach is to block password-protected archives from outside senders and scan everything else, then allow exceptions for the vendors you work with.

What should an employee do after opening a suspicious attachment?

Stop, do not click anything else, disconnect from the network if it is a laptop, and tell your IT provider right away. Speed matters more than blame, so make reporting easy and judgment-free.
