Does Microsoft 365 Back Up Your Data? The Shared Responsibility Gap Small Businesses Miss (2026)

Ulises Paiz, Owner, Ghosxt

Ulises Paiz is the owner and sole engineer at Ghosxt, a Salinas, CA managed IT and cybersecurity provider. He holds an M.S. in Cybersecurity and Information Assurance (WGU, 2026) and nine industry certifications including CompTIA SecurityX, CySA+, and Microsoft AZ-104, with prior DoD and federal contractor infrastructure experience. More about Ulises →

TL;DR: Microsoft and Google keep Microsoft 365 and Google Workspace online. Neither one is responsible for restoring your mail, files, or shared drives after an accidental delete, an insider mistake, or a ransomware attack. That's the customer's job under the shared responsibility model, and a recycle bin or retention policy alone isn't a substitute for a real backup.

Three things worth checking this week

  1. What your platform's native retention actually covers, and for how long, versus what a real backup adds on top of it.
  2. Whether a restore can reach further back than your recycle bin or retention policy window.
  3. Who owns the answer to "can we get that file back" when the request comes in after the native window has already closed.

If nobody at your business can answer question two with confidence, this is the gap to close first.

A lot of small business owners assume that because Microsoft or Google is "hosting" their email and files, those companies are also backing that data up the way an old on-site server used to get backed up. That assumption is wrong, and it's not a hidden trick, it's written into how both platforms are designed. Microsoft and Google build Microsoft 365 and Google Workspace to stay available, which is a genuinely hard infrastructure problem they solve well. Making sure your business can get back a mailbox someone deleted eight months ago, or a folder a piece of ransomware encrypted last week, is a different problem, and it's one the platform agreements place on the customer, not the provider.

1. What "shared responsibility" actually splits

The shared responsibility model is simple once it's stated plainly: the cloud provider is responsible for the platform, the customer is responsible for the data on it. Microsoft keeps Exchange Online, SharePoint, and Teams running and durable against its own outages. Google does the same for Gmail, Drive, and Shared Drives. Neither one is on the hook for a former employee's mailbox getting purged by an automated retention rule nobody remembered configuring, a shared drive folder someone deletes by mistake, or an account takeover that quietly rewrites months of files before anyone notices. Those are business risks, and under the shared responsibility model, protecting against them is the business's job.

2. Where the built-in tools stop

Both platforms ship real recovery tools, and they're useful for exactly what they're built for: undoing something recent. A deleted item sits in a recycle bin or retention window for a limited stretch, often somewhere between 30 and 90 days depending on configuration, before it's gone for good. Version history can roll a document back a few revisions. None of that is a substitute for a point-in-time backup that lives outside the platform's own retention settings. If the thing that needs restoring is older than the window, if the retention settings themselves were the thing that got misconfigured or maliciously altered, or if an entire mailbox or shared drive needs to come back exactly as it looked before an incident, native retention tools don't reach that far. That's the exact gap ransomware operators count on: encrypt or delete data, let it sit past the recovery window, and the business has nothing left to restore from.

3. What actual backup coverage looks like

A real backup for Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace keeps independent copies of mail, files, and shared content on a schedule, separate from the platform's own retention settings, so a restore can reach back to a specific date rather than being capped by whatever window happened to be configured at the time. It's one piece of the same backup discipline covered in our broader post on backup and disaster recovery for small business, applied specifically to the cloud productivity platform most small businesses now run their entire operation on. The point isn't to distrust Microsoft or Google, it's to recognize that "the data lives in the cloud" and "the data is backed up" are two different claims, and only the business itself is positioned to make the second one true.

Where to start this week

Ask whoever manages your Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace tenant one direct question: if we needed to restore an entire mailbox or shared drive to how it looked six months ago, could we actually do that today? If the honest answer depends on a recycle bin or a retention policy that was never built for that job, that's the gap to close before it gets tested by an actual incident rather than a hypothetical one.

Frequently asked questions

Doesn't Microsoft already back up my data?

Microsoft keeps the Microsoft 365 platform itself available and durable against outages on its end, which is different from restoring one deleted mailbox, one corrupted document library, or everything a ransomware attack touched months ago. That job belongs to the customer under the shared responsibility model, and the same split applies to Google Workspace.

What's the difference between a retention policy and a backup?

A retention policy or recycle bin holds deleted or changed items for a limited window, often 30 to 90 days depending on configuration, meant for quickly undoing an accidental delete. A backup keeps independent, point-in-time copies outside that window, so data can be restored to a specific date even after the native retention period has expired or been altered.

Does this apply to Google Workspace too?

Yes. Google Workspace follows the same shared responsibility split as Microsoft 365: Google is responsible for the platform's uptime and infrastructure, and the business is responsible for making sure its own mail, files, and shared drives can be restored after accidental deletion, insider action, or an account compromise.

How do I find out if my business is covered?

Ask whoever manages your IT whether Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace data is backed up separately from the platform's own retention settings, and how far back a restore can reach. If the honest answer is that recovery depends on the recycle bin or a retention policy alone, that's the gap this post is about.
