How to Report a Suspicious Email: Build a Phishing Reporting Habit at Work

Ulises Paiz, Owner, Ghosxt

Ulises Paiz is the owner and sole engineer at Ghosxt, a Salinas, CA managed IT and cybersecurity provider. He holds an M.S. in Cybersecurity and Information Assurance (WGU, 2026) and nine industry certifications including CompTIA SecurityX, CySA+, and Microsoft AZ-104, with prior DoD and federal contractor infrastructure experience. More about Ulises →

TL;DR: Most small businesses learn about a phishing email only after someone clicks. If staff report suspicious messages the moment they see them, one person's catch protects everyone. Make reporting easy, fast, and free of blame.

Five steps to take this week.

  1. Pick one place to report. A report button, a shared mailbox, or one named person.
  2. Say it out loud. Tell staff what to report and how, in two sentences.
  3. Reply to every report. Thank them and say what you found.
  4. Warn the team. If one person got it, others did too.
  5. Never punish a click. Punishment teaches people to hide mistakes.

If you run a small business in Monterey County or anywhere on the Central Coast, your best security tool is not software. It is the employee who says, "This email looks odd, can you check it?"

This guide explains why reporting beats deleting, how to set up a simple reporting habit, and what should happen in the minutes after a report arrives.

1. Why reporting beats deleting

When an employee spots a bad email and quietly deletes it, that one person is safe. Everyone else who got the same message is still at risk.

  • Attacks arrive in waves. The same message usually lands in several inboxes at once, so one report can protect the whole office.
  • Speed matters. A report in the first few minutes lets IT block the sender and pull the message from other mailboxes before anyone clicks.
  • Patterns show up. Three reports about the same fake invoice tell you a campaign is aimed at your company, not just random spam.
  • Reports reveal gaps. If a message slipped past your filters, you want to know before the next one does.

Reporting also pairs well with the other layers we recommend, such as SPF, DKIM and DMARC and phishing simulations. Filters catch most threats. Reports catch the rest.

2. Make reporting easy and safe

People report what is easy to report. If it takes five steps and a form, they will skip it.

  • Use a one-click option. Microsoft 365 and Google Workspace both offer a way to report a message from inside the inbox. Ask your IT provider to switch it on and show staff where it is.
  • Have a fallback. If the button is missing on a phone, staff can forward the message as an attachment to one shared address. Forwarding as an attachment keeps the headers that IT needs.
  • Cover texts and calls too. Tell staff the same rule applies to a strange text or phone call. See our guides to smishing and vishing.
  • Remove the fear. Say plainly that no one is in trouble for a false alarm or an honest click. Owners set this tone, and staff notice.

If someone did click, send them straight to what to do after clicking a phishing link. The first hour matters most.

3. What should happen after a report

A report that disappears into a void teaches staff to stop reporting. Close the loop every time.

  • Acknowledge it. A short reply within the hour: "Thanks, we are looking at it."
  • Check it. Look at the sender, links, and attachments without opening anything risky. Our post on risky file types lists what to be careful with.
  • Remove it everywhere. Search all mailboxes for the same message and delete it, then block the sender and any bad links.
  • Tell the team. Send a two-line heads up: what it looked like and what to do if they saw it.
  • Check for damage. If anyone interacted with it, review sign-ins and look for new forwarding rules, which we cover in hidden email forwarding rules.

This does not need a security team. A named person, a shared mailbox, and a simple checklist are enough to start.

The takeaway

A reporting habit turns every employee into a sensor. It costs nothing, it works alongside your filters, and it gets better the more it is used. Start with one reporting place and one friendly reply, and build from there. For help setting this up, see our cybersecurity and managed IT services, or check current pricing.

Frequently asked questions

How do I report a suspicious email at work?

Do not click or reply. Use the report phishing button in your email program if you have one, or forward the message as an attachment to your IT provider. Then delete it only after IT confirms they have it.

Is it bad if I clicked a link before reporting?

No. Report it anyway and say what you clicked. A fast report limits damage, and a late one does not. Nobody should be punished for an honest click.

Should employees report emails that turn out to be real?

Yes. A false alarm costs a minute. A missed phish can cost far more. Treat every report as a good call.

What is the difference between reporting an email and marking it as junk?

Junk only hides the message for you. A report tells your IT provider and your email service so the sender can be blocked and other staff can be warned.

Where do I send suspicious emails if my business has no IT department?

Pick one shared mailbox or one person, and tell everyone. If you work with an outside IT provider, send reports to them so they can check the message and block the sender.
