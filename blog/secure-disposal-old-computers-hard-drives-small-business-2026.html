How to Securely Dispose of Old Computers and Hard Drives: A Small Business Guide (2026)

TL;DR: Deleting files does not erase data. Before any computer, phone, copier, or drive leaves your office, wipe it, destroy it, or encrypt-and-erase it, then keep a record.

Three things to do this week.

  1. Find the pile. Look in closets, under desks, and in the storage room for old PCs, laptops, drives, and phones.
  2. Pick a method. Wipe working devices and destroy failed ones. Do not leave them waiting.
  3. Start a log. Record the serial number, date, method, and who did it for every device.

Most small businesses spend real effort protecting the data on computers they use every day. Then a five-year-old laptop gets replaced, someone drops it in a closet, and two years later it goes out with the recycling. Everything on it goes with it.

Old equipment is one of the quietest ways to leak data. Customer lists, tax files, saved passwords, and email archives often sit on a drive long after the machine stopped being useful. Here is how to retire equipment properly without turning it into a project.

1. Why "deleted" does not mean gone

When you delete a file or run a quick format, the system marks that space as free. The data stays until something overwrites it, and free recovery tools can often bring it back in minutes.

The risk is bigger than laptops. Plan for every device that stores data:

  • Laptops, desktops, and servers with internal drives.
  • Phones and tablets with email, photos, and business apps.
  • Copiers and printers with internal storage, as covered in our guide to printer and copier security.
  • External drives, USB sticks, and old backup media.
  • Security cameras and recorders that keep footage on disk.

If your business handles health, financial, or payment data, a lost drive may also trigger legal duties. See our overview of California breach notification.

2. Pick the right method for each device

There is no single answer. Match the method to the condition of the device and how sensitive its data was.

  • Encrypt first, then erase. If the drive was protected with BitLocker or FileVault, resetting the device and discarding the key makes the data unreadable. This is fast and is the best habit to build from day one.
  • Full wipe. For working devices you plan to resell, donate, or reuse, run the built-in reset with the full erase option, or a dedicated wiping tool. Verify that it completed.
  • Physical destruction. For failed drives, drives that will not wipe, or drives that held highly sensitive records, shred or degauss them. Do not just smash one with a hammer and assume it is done.
  • Remove and keep the drive. If a computer is being donated, pulling the drive and destroying it separately is cheap insurance.

Before wiping, make sure you actually have what you need. Confirm the data lives in your backups or cloud storage, then check that the device is clear of accounts and licenses.

3. Document it and use a trustworthy recycler

Doing it right is only half of it. You also need to prove it, especially for cyber insurance, customer questionnaires, and regulated work.

  • Keep an asset log. Record the serial number, disposal date, method, and the person responsible. Your IT asset inventory is the natural place for it.
  • Remove it from your tools. Delete the device from your management console, your identity provider, and any device management list, and clear activation locks.
  • Choose a certified recycler. Ask for a certificate of destruction or recycling for each batch, and avoid anyone who cannot explain what happens to the drives.
  • Write it into policy. Add device retirement to your data retention and destruction policy and your offboarding checklist, so returned laptops never sit unwiped.

If a device is lost or stolen rather than retired, start with our lost laptop first-hour checklist.

The takeaway

Old computers are not trash. They are filing cabinets with the label still on. Decide how each device will be erased or destroyed, do it before it leaves your hands, and write down what you did. A few minutes per device is a small price compared with explaining a data leak that started in a recycling bin.

Frequently asked questions

How do I securely wipe a business computer before disposing of it?

Back up anything you still need, then use the operating system's reset tool with full erase options or a certified wiping tool. On encrypted drives, destroying the encryption key (a cryptographic erase) is fast and effective. Always confirm the wipe finished and keep a record.

Is deleting files or formatting a drive enough?

No. Deleting files and a quick format only remove the pointers, and the data can often be recovered with free tools. Use a proper erase, cryptographic erase, or physical destruction instead.

Should I physically destroy hard drives or just wipe them?

Wiping is fine for working drives that you will resell or donate. Physically destroy any drive that is failed, cannot be wiped, or held highly sensitive data such as health records or payment information.

Can I donate or sell old business computers?

Yes, after a verified wipe, or after removing the drive and keeping it. Also sign out of accounts, remove the device from your management tools, and clear any activation locks.

What records should I keep when I dispose of IT equipment?

Keep an entry for each device with its serial number, the date, who handled it, the wipe or destruction method, and a certificate from your recycler. Auditors, insurers, and regulations such as HIPAA may ask for it.
