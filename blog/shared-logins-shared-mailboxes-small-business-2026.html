Shared Logins and Shared Mailboxes: How a Small Business Can Stop Sharing One Password

Ulises Paiz, Owner, Ghosxt

Ulises Paiz is the owner and sole engineer at Ghosxt, a Salinas, CA managed IT and cybersecurity provider. He holds an M.S. in Cybersecurity and Information Assurance (WGU, 2026) and nine industry certifications including CompTIA SecurityX, CySA+, and Microsoft AZ-104, with prior DoD and federal contractor infrastructure experience. More about Ulises →

TL;DR: One password known by many people means no accountability, no clean offboarding, and no way to tell who did what. Give everyone their own sign-in and share access, not credentials.

Three ways to stop sharing a password.

  1. Use shared mailboxes. Let named people open info@ or billing@ with their own logins.
  2. Vault the exceptions. When a tool allows one login, store it in a team password manager with MFA.
  3. Rotate on every exit. Change shared credentials the day someone leaves.

Somewhere in your office there is a sticky note, a spreadsheet, or a group text that holds the password for the front-desk email, the company Facebook page, or the accounting login. It feels harmless. Everyone needs access, and one password is easy to remember.

It is also one of the most common reasons small businesses cannot answer a simple question after an incident: who did this? Here is why shared logins cause trouble and how to replace them without slowing anyone down.

1. Why a shared password is a real risk

A password that many people know has four problems:

  • No accountability. The audit log shows that “the account” deleted a folder, not which person did.
  • Messy offboarding. When someone leaves, you must change the password and tell everyone else, and it often gets missed. See our offboarding checklist.
  • Weak MFA. Multi-factor codes go to one phone, so the team either shares that too or turns MFA off.
  • Easy to leak. The password gets pasted into chats and email, which attackers love to find after a phishing click.

2. Share the mailbox, not the password

Most shared logins are really shared inboxes: info@, support@, billing@, or orders@. These do not need a password anyone shares.

  • Microsoft 365: create a shared mailbox and grant named users Full Access and Send As. Each person opens it from their own Outlook. The shared mailbox itself usually needs no license, and its sign-in should be blocked.
  • Google Workspace: use a Google Group collaborative inbox or delegate the mailbox to specific people.

Every message is then tied to a real person, and removing someone is a single change. If you are choosing a platform, our Microsoft 365 vs Google Workspace comparison can help.

3. Vault the exceptions

Some tools, such as a social media page, a legacy vendor portal, or the bank’s business site, only allow one login. For those, use a team password manager:

  • Store the credential in a shared vault that only the right people can open.
  • Keep the MFA code with a manager or in the vault, never on a personal phone.
  • Rotate the password whenever someone with access leaves or changes roles.
  • Where the tool offers it, add people as named users instead. Our guide to single sign-on covers the next step up.

Admin accounts deserve extra care. Never share a global administrator login; see privileged access management.

The takeaway

Shared passwords save a minute today and cost hours after the first incident or departure. Give every person their own sign-in, convert shared inboxes into shared mailboxes, and keep the few unavoidable shared credentials in a vault with MFA. You will know who did what, and removing access becomes a five-minute task.

Frequently asked questions

Is it ever okay to share a password at work?

Avoid it wherever you can. If a tool truly allows only one login, store the credential in a team password manager, turn on multi-factor authentication, restrict who can view it, and change it whenever someone with access leaves.

What is the difference between a shared mailbox and a shared account?

A shared mailbox, such as info@ or billing@, lets several named people read and send from one address using their own sign-ins. A shared account is one login with one password that everyone knows. The mailbox is safe and auditable; the shared account is neither.

Does a Microsoft 365 shared mailbox need a license?

Usually not. A shared mailbox under 50 GB does not need a license, but each person you give access to does need their own licensed account. Adding an archive or exceeding the size limit changes that, so check current Microsoft licensing.

How do I share a Google Workspace inbox like support@ safely?

Use a Google Group set up as a collaborative inbox, or delegate the mailbox to named users. Either way, each person signs in as themselves and the shared address never has a password the whole team knows.

How do I stop sharing a social media or software login that only has one user?

Put the credentials in a team password manager with shared vaults, protect the account with an authenticator app held by a manager, and where the tool supports it, add each person as a named user or admin instead.
