Sign-In Alert You Don't Recognize? What to Do Next

Ulises Paiz, Owner, Ghosxt

Ulises Paiz is the owner and sole engineer at Ghosxt, a Salinas, CA managed IT and cybersecurity provider. He holds an M.S. in Cybersecurity and Information Assurance (WGU, 2026) and nine industry certifications including CompTIA SecurityX, CySA+, and Microsoft AZ-104, with prior DoD and federal contractor infrastructure experience. More about Ulises →

TL;DR: A "new sign-in" or "unusual sign-in activity" alert you do not recognize means either someone has your password or someone is pretending to be the alert. Do not click the link in the message. Open your account directly, check recent activity, and if the sign-in is not yours, change the password, sign out every session, and check your MFA methods and mail rules.

Five steps for the first 15 minutes.

  1. Skip the link. Open Microsoft 365 or Google in a new tab and sign in normally.
  2. Check recent activity. Compare the time, device and location to what you were doing.
  3. Change the password. From a device you trust, to one you have never used before.
  4. Sign out everywhere. Kill every open session, not just the one you see.
  5. Check what changed. MFA methods, forwarding, inbox rules, and recovery details.

An email or phone notice says someone just signed in to your work account from a device or place you do not recognize. For a small business in Monterey County or anywhere on the Central Coast, that one alert is often the only warning you get before an attacker reads your mail, sends invoices as you, or locks you out. This guide explains what an unrecognized sign-in alert means, what to do in the first 15 minutes, and how to stop the next one from mattering.

The blind spot is that most people do one of two things: ignore the alert, or click the button inside it. Both can go wrong.

1. First, make sure the alert itself is real

Attackers know people panic when they see "unusual sign-in activity," so fake security alerts are one of the most common phishing lures. The fake version links to a lookalike login page that captures your password and your MFA code.

  • Never use the link or button in the alert. Open a new browser tab, go to your Microsoft 365 or Google account the way you always do, and look at recent activity there.
  • If the sign-in shows up in your account, the alert was real. If nothing matches, the message was likely phishing. Report it with your phishing report button and delete it.
  • Already clicked and typed your password? Treat it as a real compromise and follow the steps below. Our guide on what to do after clicking a phishing link covers the rest.

2. Read the details before you decide

Not every strange alert is an attack. Location is estimated from an IP address, and it is often wrong by a city or two.

  • Usually harmless: a nearby city, your phone switching to cellular data, a new laptop you just set up, or a sign-in while you were traveling.
  • Worth acting on now: another country, a device type nobody on your team uses, a time when you were asleep, or several alerts in a row.
  • Most serious: the alert says the sign-in succeeded and MFA was satisfied. That means the attacker got past your second factor, often by stealing a session cookie or tricking someone into approving a prompt.

A blocked attempt with the wrong password is still a signal. It means your address and possibly an old password are on someone's list, and it is a good time to check for password spraying against the rest of your team.

3. If the sign-in is not yours, lock it down

Do these in order, from a computer or phone you trust:

  • Change the password to a long one you have never used anywhere else. A password manager makes this easy.
  • Sign out of all sessions. A password change alone may not remove someone who is already signed in. Use the "sign out everywhere" option, and have your admin revoke sessions from the admin console.
  • Check your MFA methods. Remove any phone number, authenticator or device you do not recognize. Attackers often add their own so they can come back.
  • Look for mail rules and forwarding. A hidden forwarding rule is the most common thing left behind after an email takeover.
  • Tell your IT contact and your team. If the account sent anything while it was compromised, customers and vendors may need a heads-up before a fake invoice gets paid.

Then think one step back. If the attacker had a working password, ask where it came from: a reused password, a phishing page, or malware that steals saved browser passwords. If it is malware, changing the password from the infected computer does not help until that computer is cleaned.

How to make the next alert less scary

Alerts are a smoke detector. You still want fewer fires.

  • Turn on multi-factor authentication for every account, and move to passkeys where you can.
  • Use conditional access to block sign-ins from countries where nobody on your team works.
  • Make sure alerts go to someone who reads them, and keep sign-in logs long enough to look back when something happens.

The takeaway

An unrecognized sign-in alert is a gift if you act on it and a liability if you ignore it or click it. Check it from your account, not the message. If it is not yours, change the password, sign out everywhere, and check what the intruder changed. Then close the gap so the next attempt fails. Frequently asked questions

Is an unusual sign-in activity email real or a scam?

It can be either. Real alerts come from Microsoft or Google, but scammers copy them to steal passwords. Never use the link in the message. Open your account in a new browser tab, sign in the way you normally do, and check recent activity there. If the alert is real, the same sign-in will show up.

What should I do if someone signed in to my work email from another location?

Change the password from a device you trust, sign out every other session, and check that your multi-factor authentication methods are yours. Then look for new inbox rules or forwarding, and tell whoever manages your IT so they can review the sign-in logs.

Why does my sign-in alert show a city I have never been to?

Location comes from an IP address, and that is often approximate. A phone on cellular data, a VPN, or an internet provider routing through another city can all show a strange location. A foreign country, an unknown device, or a time you were asleep is a stronger sign of a real problem.

Does changing my password stop someone who already signed in?

Not always on its own. An attacker who stole a session cookie can stay signed in after a password change. After the change, use the option to sign out of all sessions, and have an admin revoke sessions from the admin console.

How do I stop getting sign-in alerts from other countries?

Turn on multi-factor authentication for every account, use phishing-resistant methods such as passkeys where you can, and have your admin block sign-ins from countries where nobody on your team works. The alerts may still come, but the attempts will fail.
