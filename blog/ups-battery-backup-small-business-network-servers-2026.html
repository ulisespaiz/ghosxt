Do You Need a UPS? Battery Backup for Small Business Servers and Networks (2026)

TL;DR: A surge protector blocks spikes. A UPS keeps your server, firewall, and network running through short outages and gives them time to shut down cleanly. Protect the infrastructure first.

Three things to do this week.

  1. List your critical gear. Server, NAS, firewall, switch, modem, and access points.
  2. Check what is plugged into a UPS. Look at the label and the battery age on any unit you already own.
  3. Run a test. Do a controlled pull-the-plug test after hours and note how long it lasts.

Most small offices lose power for a few seconds at a time, not for hours. A flicker, a brownout on a hot afternoon, or a neighbor's work crew hitting a line. The lights blink and come back, and nobody thinks about it.

Your servers and network equipment do notice. A sudden power loss can corrupt a database, crash a file server, or leave the internet down until someone power-cycles the modem. A UPS is a cheap way to avoid all of it. Here is what it does and how to choose one.

1. What a UPS does (and what a surge protector does not)

A surge protector is a power strip with a part that absorbs voltage spikes. It has no battery, so when the power drops, everything behind it drops too.

A UPS (uninterruptible power supply) adds a battery and does three jobs:

  • Rides through outages. Equipment keeps running on battery, usually for 5 to 15 minutes at a typical small office load.
  • Smooths bad power. It filters sags, brownouts, and spikes before they reach your gear.
  • Enables a clean shutdown. With a USB or network connection, the UPS tells the server to power down safely before the battery runs out.

That last point matters most. Downtime from a hard crash can cost far more than the hardware, as we break down in our look at the real cost of downtime.

2. What to put on a UPS first

You do not need to protect every desk. Start with the equipment that everyone else depends on:

  • Servers and NAS devices that hold files, databases, or line-of-business software.
  • The firewall, switches, and modem. If these stay up, the internet and phones can survive a short blip. See our guide to business firewalls.
  • Wi-Fi access points and VoIP gear if they are powered from a switch or injector.
  • Point-of-sale terminals and critical workstations where unsaved work or open transactions are at stake.

Leave laser printers and space heaters off the UPS. They draw large bursts of power and can overload the unit. Where the gear lives also matters, so read our tips on network closet and server room protection.

3. How to size, maintain, and test it

Pick a unit based on the load you actually have, not the number on the box.

  • Add up the watts. Total the draw of everything you plan to plug in, then leave about 30 percent headroom. Check the unit's watt rating, not just its VA number.
  • Choose the type. Line-interactive units suit most small offices. Online (double-conversion) models cost more and make sense for servers and sensitive equipment.
  • Plan for the battery. Replace it every 3 to 5 years and heat shortens that. Label each unit with its install date.
  • Test twice a year. Pull the plug after hours and confirm the runtime and the automatic shutdown actually work.
  • Monitor it. Have the UPS send alerts when it switches to battery or reports a fault, so someone knows before the lights go out for good.

For outages that last hours rather than minutes, a UPS is only the bridge. Pair it with a plan, as we describe in our PG&E shutoff IT continuity guide, and make sure your backups are solid either way.

The takeaway

A UPS is one of the least glamorous and most useful purchases in IT. For a few hundred dollars, it turns a sudden crash into a clean shutdown and keeps your internet up through the blips that happen every month. Protect the servers and network gear first, size for the real load, replace batteries on schedule, and test it twice a year.

Frequently asked questions

What does a UPS do for a small business?

A UPS (uninterruptible power supply) keeps equipment running on battery for a few minutes when power drops, and it cleans up sags and spikes. That gives servers and network gear time to shut down safely instead of crashing mid-write.

Is a UPS the same as a surge protector?

No. A surge protector only blocks voltage spikes and has no battery. A UPS adds battery power and voltage regulation, so equipment stays on during a blackout or brownout.

What equipment should be on a UPS?

Put servers, NAS devices, the firewall, switches, the modem or ISP handoff, and Wi-Fi access points on a UPS. Workstations can use one if the person works with unsaved data, but infrastructure comes first.

How long will a UPS keep my equipment running?

Most small office units give 5 to 15 minutes at typical load. That is meant for a clean shutdown or to ride out a brief outage, not to run the office for hours. Longer outages need a generator or a larger battery system.

How often should I replace a UPS battery?

Plan on replacing the battery every 3 to 5 years, or sooner if the unit reports a fault. Run a controlled test every six months so a dead battery does not surprise you during a real outage.
