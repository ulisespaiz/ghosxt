Written Information Security Program (WISP): What a Small Business Actually Needs (2026)

Ulises Paiz, Owner, Ghosxt

Ulises Paiz is the owner and sole engineer at Ghosxt, a Salinas, CA managed IT and cybersecurity provider. He holds an M.S. in Cybersecurity and Information Assurance (WGU, 2026) and nine industry certifications including CompTIA SecurityX, CySA+, and Microsoft AZ-104, with prior DoD and federal contractor infrastructure experience. More about Ulises →

TL;DR: A WISP is the written plan describing how a business protects sensitive data. Insurers, vendors, and clients are asking for one more often. It needs a data inventory, named safeguards, and an owner, not thirty pages of legal boilerplate.

A usable WISP needs three things, not thirty.

  1. A plain inventory of what sensitive data you hold and where it lives, so the rest of the document has something real to protect.
  2. Named administrative, technical, and physical safeguards that match what the business actually does, not a generic template.
  3. A named owner and a review date, so it gets updated instead of sitting unread until an insurer or auditor asks for it.

If your business has never been asked for one, it likely will be soon. Renewal applications and vendor questionnaires are asking more often, not less.

A vendor security questionnaire lands in the inbox and one line stops the process cold: "Please attach your Written Information Security Program." Nobody on staff is sure what that means or whether one exists. That moment is becoming more common, not less. Cyber insurance renewals, client contracts, and vendor onboarding forms are increasingly asking small businesses to produce a WISP, whether or not the business's home state legally requires one. A WISP is not a piece of security software or a single control you turn on. It is a written document, and if it does not exist, the honest answer to "do you have one" is no.

1. A WISP is a written plan, not a checkbox

A written information security program is the document that describes how a business protects sensitive data: what data it holds, what administrative, technical, and physical safeguards are in place, and who is responsible for keeping them up. It is not itself a technical control, in the way multi-factor authentication or patching is. It is the plan that ties those controls together into something a business can point to and say "here is how we protect data," rather than relying on an unwritten assumption that everyone is doing the right thing. A WISP is closely related to, but not the same as, an incident response plan. The WISP describes how data is protected day to day; the incident response plan, referenced inside it, describes what happens when that protection fails.

2. What actually belongs in one

A WISP that is copied from a generic template rarely reflects how a specific business actually operates, so it needs a few concrete pieces rather than vague language about taking security seriously. Start with a plain inventory: what categories of sensitive data the business holds, such as employee records, client contact and payment information, or anything under an NDA, and roughly where that data lives. From there, name the safeguards in three categories. Administrative safeguards cover who is responsible for security decisions and how access is granted and removed. Technical safeguards cover the controls actually protecting the data day to day, such as patching and backups. Physical safeguards cover device and facility controls, like what happens to a laptop that leaves the office. A WISP should also state how long different categories of data are kept and how they are destroyed when no longer needed [VERIFY exact retention schedule against your business's legal and contractual obligations], and how third parties who touch that data are vetted before they get access to it, a topic covered in more depth in our vendor and third-party risk post.

3. Why this keeps showing up in more places

A WISP started as a state-law requirement in a handful of jurisdictions, but it has spread well beyond that. Cyber insurance renewal applications are asking for one more often, and a missing WISP can affect underwriting or pricing. Client and vendor security questionnaires increasingly list it as a required attachment before a contract gets signed. The practical result is that a WISP has become baseline documentation a small business should have on hand, independent of whether its home state's law technically requires one. Waiting until a renewal application or a vendor questionnaire forces the question is the expensive way to find out you do not have one.

Where to start this week

If nothing exists in writing yet, start with the data inventory. List the categories of sensitive data the business actually holds and where each one lives, then write down the administrative, technical, and physical safeguards already in place around that data, even informally. Name one owner and put a review date on the calendar, at least once a year. A WISP is also one piece of a broader written policy suite, alongside an incident response plan, a business continuity and disaster recovery plan, an access control and acceptable use policy, a retention and destruction policy, and an AI acceptable use policy, that a managed IT relationship should already be maintaining for you during onboarding, along with an annual independent risk assessment arranged through a third-party assessor, rather than leaving you to draft the whole stack alone.

Frequently asked questions

What is a WISP?

A written information security program (WISP) is a document describing the administrative, technical, and physical safeguards a business uses to protect sensitive data. It is not a single control, it is the plan that ties the individual controls together and names who is responsible for them.

Does a small business need a WISP if it doesn't handle health or financial data directly?

Most small businesses hold some category of sensitive data, such as employee records or client contact and payment information, even without realizing it. Increasingly, cyber insurance renewal applications and vendor or client security questionnaires ask whether a WISP exists regardless of industry.

How is a WISP different from an incident response plan?

A WISP is the umbrella document describing how data is protected day to day. An incident response plan is one piece referenced inside it, describing what happens when that protection fails. A business should have both, and they should point to each other.

Who should own the WISP and how often should it be reviewed?

One named person, typically the owner or whoever owns IT and compliance decisions, should hold the WISP and review it at least once a year, and after any significant change to how the business handles data or which vendors touch it.
