Year-End SaaS Audit: Cut Subscription Waste and Close the Security Gaps It Hides

Ulises Paiz, Owner, Ghosxt

Ulises Paiz is the owner and sole engineer at Ghosxt, a Salinas, CA managed IT and cybersecurity provider. He holds an M.S. in Cybersecurity and Information Assurance (WGU, 2026) and nine industry certifications including CompTIA SecurityX, CySA+, and Microsoft AZ-104, with prior DoD and federal contractor infrastructure experience. More about Ulises →

TL;DR: Most small businesses have no single list of every SaaS app connected to their business, and every forgotten account is both wasted spend and unwatched attack surface. Before next year's budget locks in, pull the real list, check it against who's still employed, and cut what nobody uses.

What that means in practice.

  1. Pull the real list, not a best guess. Your Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace admin console already knows which apps have sign-in access.
  2. Match access against the current roster. Anyone no longer employed should not still show up with live access to anything.
  3. Decide per app: keep, consolidate, or cut. "Nobody remembers signing up for this" is a decision point, not something to skip past.
  4. Put a recurring date on it. A one-time cleanup drifts right back to where it started within a year.

None of this requires new software or a big project. It requires two lists that already exist and an afternoon to compare them.

Budget season and year-end cleanup tend to land on the same few weeks, and that overlap is useful. Most small businesses can name their big software costs, the accounting platform, the main productivity suite, but nobody actually owns the full list of every SaaS app with access to company data. Apps get added one at a time, a trial converts to paid without anyone noticing, an employee signs up for a tool with a company card to solve one problem and never mentions it again. None of that is reckless. It's just how software gets adopted when there's no single person whose job is tracking it. The fix isn't complicated, but it does need to actually happen instead of staying on next quarter's list.

The list is longer than anyone thinks

Ask an owner how many SaaS tools the business pays for and the answer is almost always a significant undercount. Beyond the handful everyone uses daily, there's usually a scheduling tool one person set up, a design app a former employee subscribed to and never canceled, a free trial that quietly started billing, and a vendor's portal that technically counts as software with access to your data even though nobody thinks of it that way. None of these show up on a mental list because no one was ever asked to make one. The problem isn't any single subscription. It's that the master list doesn't exist anywhere.

Every forgotten account is a door nobody's watching

Most SaaS tools today are set up with "sign in with Microsoft" or "sign in with Google" instead of a separate password, which is convenient and also means the app now holds a standing grant of access to your identity provider. That grant doesn't expire when interest in the app does, and it doesn't get revoked when an employee leaves unless someone remembers it exists, which is exactly the gap covered in our piece on OAuth app phishing. It's also why a SaaS audit belongs next to, not instead of, a proper offboarding checklist: an app that was never centrally provisioned in the first place usually isn't on that checklist either.

How to actually run the audit

Start with your identity provider's admin console, Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace, both keep a list of every third-party app that's been granted sign-in access, and it's almost always longer than expected. Pull it alongside a scan of recurring charges on the card or cards used for software. Cross-reference both lists against who is currently employed and which apps still have an obvious, active owner. For everything else, make an actual decision: keep it because it's genuinely used, consolidate it because another tool already does the same job, or cut it and explicitly revoke its access rather than assuming an unused app is a harmless one.

Make it a quarter, not a one-time project

The first pass through this is the hard one, since the backlog has usually built up over a year or more. After that, the same review takes far less time if it's done on a recurring schedule instead of waiting for the next big cleanup. Tying it to a slow point on the calendar, or running it alongside every new hire and every departure, keeps the list from drifting back into the same mess a few years from now.

Frequently asked questions

We don't have a dedicated IT department. Is a SaaS audit realistic for a small business?

Yes. Most of the work is pulling two lists that already exist, the connected-app report from your identity provider and the recurring charges on your card statement, and comparing both against who is actually employed today. For a business with a handful of employees, that comparison is an afternoon, not a project.

Isn't this really a budgeting exercise rather than a security one?

It's both, and treating it as only a budget exercise is how the security half gets skipped. An account nobody remembers granting access to is exactly the kind of gap that stays invisible until an incident forces the question of what actually had access to company email or files.

What's the single easiest place to start?

The admin console for whichever platform is your identity provider, Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace. Both list every third-party app that's been granted sign-in access, which is usually a longer and more surprising list than the one in anyone's head.

How often should this happen after the first cleanup?

Quarterly is a reasonable cadence once the initial backlog is cleared. Tying it to a slow point in the calendar, or to whenever a new employee is onboarded, keeps it from sliding back into a once-every-few-years scramble.
